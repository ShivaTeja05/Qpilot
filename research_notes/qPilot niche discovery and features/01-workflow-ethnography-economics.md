## 1. How this corner of the niche works

### 1.0 The one-paragraph picture

RT-qPCR (reverse-transcription quantitative PCR) is the default way a wet lab measures how much messenger RNA of a few named genes is present in a set of samples. A study is "measure genes X (typically 3–20 targets plus 1–3 reference genes) in samples Y (typically 6–60 biological samples)". It is still one of the most-used methods in biology: about 5,000 new PubMed Central papers per month mention "qPCR" in their Methods [5], and my own PubMed title/abstract query (qPCR OR RT-qPCR OR "real-time PCR" OR "quantitative PCR" OR "real-time quantitative PCR") returns 12,900 records for 2015, 16,827 for 2019, 19,510 for 2024 and 22,390 for 2025 [85]. The people are PhD students, postdocs and technicians in academic labs, scientists in biotech R&D, and staff in core facilities. The week runs: design → RNA extraction → RNA quality check → reverse transcription (RT) to cDNA → plate setup → 2-hour instrument run → quality check of the run → analysis (mostly Excel, sometimes Prism/R) → statistics → figure → write-up/decision. The two places where time, money and confidence leak are (a) the end-of-run judgment ("is this run usable or do I repeat it?") and (b) the hand-made spreadsheet analysis, which is invisible to reviewers: a 2026 audit of 432 papers in top genetics and plant-science journals concluded that "due to inadequate reporting, it is impossible to determine whether >90% of qPCR results are reliable" [2].

### 1.3 Vocabulary the developers will meet

- **Cq / Ct / Cp**: cycle at which fluorescence crosses a threshold; later = less starting material. MIQE says use "Cq" [12].
- **NTC**: no-template control (water); should not amplify; Thermo "guarantee that assays run in a NTC reaction will not produce detectable amplification signal (Ct > 38)" [19]. **NRT / -RT / NAC**: no-reverse-transcriptase control, detects genomic DNA; MIQE 2.0: essential when first extracting RNA, "desirable, but no longer essential" once validated DNA-free [4].
- **IRC / IPC**: inter-run (inter-plate) calibrator, a common sample on every plate to remove plate-to-plate offset; qBase manual recommends "at least 3 inter-run calibrators" [25]; TATAA sells a synthetic one [26].
- **Technical vs biological replicate**: same cDNA pipetted twice/thrice vs different animals/dishes. The instrument's "Ct Mean averages only technical replicates" [63].
- **ΔCq, ΔΔCq, 2^-ΔΔCq (Livak)**: normalise to reference gene, then to control group, then exponentiate assuming 100% efficiency; **Pfaffl**: same with measured efficiencies [29].
- **Efficiency (E)**: fold increase per cycle from a dilution series slope, E = 10^(-1/slope); 90–110% (slope −3.58 to −3.10) and R² ≥ 0.98 are the usual acceptance window [21][19][29]. Per-well efficiency from the curve is what LinRegPCR does [12].
- **Melt curve / Tm**: SYBR assays end with a temperature ramp; one sharp peak = one product; primer-dimer peaks sit 5–10 °C lower [65].
- **Sample-maximisation vs primer-maximisation layout**: all samples for one gene on one plate (preferred for ΔΔCq, avoids plate effects) vs all genes for a subset of samples (needs IRC) [27].
- **Flags**: QuantStudio exports NOAMP, EXPFAIL, HIGHSD columns; "the flags are advisory, not definitive" [63].
- **RDML / RDES**: Real-time PCR Data Markup Language (XML, raw curves + setup) and the Essential Spreadsheet Format (CSV) proposed for submission [9][4].
- **MIQE / MIQE 2.0**: the reporting checklist (2009; revised 2025, Clinical Chemistry) [4]; the 2009 version has "85 elements (57 essential, 28 desirable)" [2].

### 1.4 Standards and what "good" looks like

- MIQE 2.0 (2025) says Cq values "should be converted into efficiency-corrected target quantities and reported with prediction intervals"; it lists four analysis steps that change the observed Cq (how baseline is subtracted; whether a threshold is used; whether thresholds are consistent across runs; whether smoothing is applied); it asks for "Storage and submission of raw fluorescence data using RDES or RDML", "Method of baseline correction and Cq determination", "qPCR analysis program (source, version)", "Frequency and Cq of negative controls", "PCR efficiency estimation and method", "Method of validation of reference genes", "Choice of significance level and calculation of statistical power", and lot numbers for RT reagents and qPCR buffer/kit [4]. It also states that averaging Cq values is a geometric mean of quantities and that "statistical tests on Cq values should be avoided" [4].
- Vendor acceptance rules practitioners actually use: NTC "no Cq value determined. Otherwise, very high Cq values (>38 cycles) may be acceptable, as long as they are not within 3 cycles of the" lowest standard; "A data point should be excluded if the standard deviation of Cq > 0.5"; r² ≥ 0.98; efficiency 90–110% [21]. Thermo: efficiency 90–100%, slope −3.6 to −3.3; 10-fold dilutions ~3.3 cycles apart [19].
- A core facility's internal standard: "90% or greater is an accepted norm" for efficiency; "Reagents from different lot numbers" must not be mixed; "each new lot of enzyme should be compared with old lots using known controls"; failed negative controls mean "all samples associated with the failed controls should be considered invalid" and re-run; raw data "including fluorescence growth curves, the fluorescence threshold values, should be maintained for 5 years" [30].

### 1.5 Instruments, software and formats in use

- Instruments: Applied Biosystems QuantStudio 3/5/6/7 Pro (Thermo), StepOnePlus (older), Bio-Rad CFX96/384 and CFX Opus, Roche LightCycler, Bio-Rad iCycler/iQ5 (legacy, still in SOPs [29]). Cores list QuantStudio 5 [36], QuantStudio 6 Pro [35], CFX96/384 and QuantStudio 7 Pro [38], ViiA 7 [35].
- Vendor software: QuantStudio Design & Analysis v2 (desktop and Connect cloud; a command-line application exists that can export "data for all experiments into one single XLS or XLSX file" [22]); CFX Maestro (gene-study window; ResearchGate users report it treating same-named biological replicates as technical replicates, no passive-reference option, and the need to switch off baseline subtraction before exporting for LinRegPCR [55][47]). ResearchGate's topic pages hold 87 Maestro and 189 CFX questions [55].
- Exports: .xlsx is the de-facto interchange format; Benchling's QuantStudio connector accepts only .xlsx/.xls, "Processing of the .csv and .txt formats are not currently supported" [23]. RDML export exists but is barely used [5].
- Analysis tools people reach for: Excel templates (ExcelDemy tutorial [68]; core-provided "QPCR analysis template.xls" [91]); GraphPad Prism; vendor gene-study modules; free tools that mostly take Cq tables (biotools.fr web calculator [69]; qRAT, MIT, Windows, CSV/TXT in [8]; PlateVision R, GPL-3, Dec 2025, plate heatmaps from the "'Excel' export" [16]; qLOOK R for .EDS files [15]; tidyqpcr [17]; personal scripts "I wouldn't want to plot all these on excel!" [18]). Hampton et al.: "Many laboratories currently use software such as GraphPad Prism or Excel" and "software-derived CT values" from different manufacturers "often yield substantially different results" [5].

### 1.6 Money and time (economics)

**Instruments.** Fisher Scientific lists the QuantStudio 5 96-well 0.2 mL (A28139) at $66,500 (search snippet of the product page; the page itself timed out for me) [72]; a reseller quotes an MSRP of $51,938.43 for the 0.1 mL variant and used units at $7,199–12,850 [73]; an older comparison put Bio-Rad CFX at ~$46–47k vs ~$55k for QS5 [74]. A buyer's guide bands 384-well/high-throughput systems at $35,000–55,000+ and mid-range 96-well at $10,000–20,000 [75].

**Service contracts.** 10–15% of list price per year by rule of thumb (≈$5,000/yr on a $50k instrument); a single optical repair $7,000–10,000 [75]; other guides give $1,000–5,000/yr [76].

**Consumables.** Master-mix list prices work out to ~$0.30–0.75 per 20 µL SYBR reaction (vendor pages; my arithmetic) [86]; one cost model gives $85.78 per 96-well plate ($0.89/reaction) and $167.86 per 384-well plate ($0.44/reaction), with plates $5.55 (96) and $8.12 (384) [75]. Lab Manager's buyer survey (194 respondents; undated page): reagent cost per well is <$0.25 for 10%, $0.25–0.50 for 17%, $0.76–1.00 for 17%, >$1.50 for 12%, and 28% "don't know" [81].

**Core facility prices (what a plate costs when you don't own the machine).** UC Davis: $30 internal / $45 external to run a client-pipetted 384-well plate (snippet) [31]; MIT (2012 page): $30/$40 per 384 plate [41]; Brown FY26: QuantStudio 6 run $56 internal / $100 external academic [35]; UTHSC: machine use free, $8 per 96- or 384-well plate, SYBR $49.50/vial [39]; UMMC: $2 per sample on CFX, $3 on QuantStudio 7 Pro, plus one-time primer charge [38]; Texas A&M–CC: SYBR full 96-well plate $1.04/well self-service to $2.49/well external, TaqMan $2.84–6.55/well, "without oligos", bioinformatics $85–115/h [37]; EPFL: robotised 384-well plate assembly 35 CHF, instrument booking per hour, Fluidigm BioMark on demand [40].

**Software.** Vendor desktop analysis software is bundled with instruments; a 21 CFR Part 11 compliance module is quoted at ~$5,860 per licence and cloud subscriptions at $0–3,000+/user/yr [75] (vendor-blog figures, unattributed). Clarida's lab tier is €4,800–9,600/yr (brief). I found no verified price for CFX Maestro or GenEx (gap).

**Labour.** Technician pay $18–36/h in US adverts [84]. Estimate: a failed 96-well plate costs 3–5 person-hours (setup, run supervision, QC, re-analysis) plus $60–120 reagents and plasticware, i.e. roughly $150–300 per repeat before sample cost; a TaqMan plate roughly double on reagents. Treat as estimate.

**Market size.** qPCR instruments ~$1.2B in 2025, benchtop 58.4%, clinical diagnostics 36.8% of application share [77]; the whole real-time PCR market ~$6.35B in 2024 [78]; reagents and consumables 61.2% of a combined dPCR+qPCR market in 2025, pharma/biotech end users 37.4% [79]. Installed base: one aggregator says ">250,000 units", another "80,000+"; neither shows a method; unreliable [80]. A 2012 survey of 363 research respondents found 86% use qPCR [82]; a ~2016 vendor-market survey had 52 responses, 24% from PI labs and 41% from core labs [83].

**Papers per year.** 22,390 PubMed records in 2025 by my title/abstract query [85]; Untergasser et al. counted ~22,000 PubMed papers on quantitative/real-time PCR in 2021, "about 60 per day" [9]; ~5,000 PMC Methods mentions per month [5].

### 1.7 Where the decisions are made (and by whom)

1. **Baseline and threshold** (software, usually automatic): "Auto-baseline can fail silently: 'the Ct values just shift by half a cycle and you never notice'" [63]; observed Cq "increase linearly when the threshold is set at a higher level"; automatic thresholds are often "10 standard deviations (SD) above the mean ground phase fluorescence" [6]; MIQE 2.0 lists the four analysis choices that move Cq [4].
2. **Replicate outlier exclusion** (analyst, by eye): rules range from 0.2–0.5 Ct [44][45], "<1.5 difference. Omit outliers" (lab protocol) [33], an SD>0.5 exclusion (Promega) [21], qRAT default SD 0.3 [8], to a Cq-dependent table (0.5 at Cq 25–31 rising to 1.9 at Cq 35) [34]. Ruiz-Villalba et al. argue the 0.5-cycle rule "suits high-copy samples only" and that up to Cq 35 "a spread of up to two cycles should be acceptable" [6].
3. **NTC amplification** (analyst): common rules are "at least 5 cycles later than your lowest-abundance sample" and "within 3–4 cycles, stop and troubleshoot" [65]; ResearchGate answers: NTC ">38 cycles but your actual product is coming in @ <30 cycles ... will have no impact" [50][51]; vendor: Ct>38 is clean [19][21].
4. **Reference gene** (habit): GAPDH/ACTB "usually" [28]; single unvalidated reference gives "erroneous normalisation up to threefold and 6-fold in 25% and 10% of the cases" [11]; gene-stability M-values reported in 0.4% of papers [2].
5. **Repeat or not** (student with PI): a lab notebook flagged 17 samples with replicate Ct differences >1 and "called for a full rerun"; another run had 19 of 24 samples with that gap [70]. A core's rule: failed controls invalidate all associated samples, which are re-run after the cause is documented [30].
6. **Claim size** (PI/reviewer): "Fold-changes of 1.2- or 1.5-fold are routinely reported as biologically meaningful" [3]; Bustin 2025 proposes two-fold is reliable only at Cq<25, ≥3-fold at Cq 25–30, ≥4-fold above Cq 30, and that detecting a 1.5-fold change with SD 0.5 Cq needs six technical replicates at 80% power [10].
7. **Which genes to validate after RNA-seq** (PI): "no rule of thumb"; vendors say 5–20 or "5-84 genes"; a Biostars answer suggests ≥20 genes with Pearson r ≥ 0.7 [88][62]; "restricted to maybe a dozen genes per run" [61].

### 1.8 What goes wrong (a short catalogue, each with evidence)

- Efficiency assumed, not measured: in a 20-paper survey "none mentioned amplification efficiency" [5]; >90% of 432 papers "did not mention testing efficiency or specificity" [2]; assuming E=2 at Cq 28 with true E=1.7 makes the quantity "100 times lower than it actually is" [6]; "differences of as little as 3%" in efficiency can change ratios by 200% after 30 cycles [5].
- Master-mix and lot effects: four master mixes on the same cDNA gave ΔCq spreads translating to "around 10- to about 30-fold" differences for nine targets; two master mixes on two RNA samples gave fold differences varying "between two- and fivefold" [11]; MIQE 2.0 asks that assays be "verified when changing batches" [4]; a core compares each new enzyme lot against old lots with known controls [30].
- Plate-to-plate offset: "the 6-times difference between runs 2 and 5" in a multi-plate experiment [7]; calibrator errors "propagate to every non-calibrator sample" [7].
- Instrument-to-instrument shift: pooled ΔCq of 1.5 (~2.9-fold) between instruments [10].
- Low-copy noise: Poisson variation exceeds pipetting error "below 100 target copies per reaction"; at 10 copies CV 33% [6]; "95% CI of 0.71 to 14.74" for a 3× comparison above Cq 30 [10].
- Spreadsheet damage: 30.9% of 11,117 PMC articles with supplementary Excel gene lists contain autocorrected gene names (2014–2020), up from 19.6% in 2005–2015 [13][14].
- Lost provenance: no paper of 432 mentioned RDML [2]; "fewer than 10 researchers have deposited RDML files" [5]; "if the template amount cannot be reconstructed from the ELN entry, the run is not really documented" [32].

### 1.10 Key-question digest (takeaway, inferences, gaps per question)

**Q1. Workflow, time per step, tools, handoffs.**
Takeaway: a single plate is a 1.5-day loop (extraction + RT one day, plate + 2 h run + analysis the next); multi-plate studies are serialised by one person's bench time and the instrument booking. Cited findings: table 1.2 [20][27][28][29][40][63]. Inferences: analysis time per plate is unmeasured anywhere I could find; the cost of the end-of-run judgment is paid in repeat plates rather than in analysis minutes. Gaps: no source gives measured hours for plate setup or analysis; no ethnographic study of qPCR work exists in the literature I reached.

**Q2. Fraction of runs repeated and why; cost of a repeat.**
Takeaway: no published repeat rate for research qPCR exists; proxies suggest partial repeats are routine. Cited findings: full reruns in open notebooks [70]; "Dropping replicates in more than 5–10% of wells is described as a sign of a systematic workflow problem" and "about 80% of excessive replicate variability comes from three sources: pipetting, bubbles/sealing, and low template abundance" (vendor blog, author's experience) [64]; core rule that failed controls invalidate associated samples [30]; "the first run completely failed, but the second run was successful" (notebook) [70]. Inferences: because reruns are triggered by thresholds (0.5 Ct, NTC Ct) rather than by consequence, many reruns are unnecessary for the conclusion and some necessary ones are skipped; cost per repeat is roughly $150–300 plus sample (estimate, Section 1.6). Gaps: no survey or audit of research-lab repeat rates; clinical "invalid run rate" literature not reached.

**Q3. What people do in Excel; outlier rules; NTC handling; lot tracking.**
Takeaway: Excel is the analysis engine and the provenance black hole. Cited findings: pivot/average/propagate by hand, typos break analysis [63]; copy/paste tools where "calculation errors" go "unnoticed" [8]; gene-name autocorrect 30.9% [13]; outlier rules in 1.7(2); NTC rules in 1.7(3); lot tracking only in a core's logbooks ("Product name... Manufacturer... Product number... Sequence... Concentration") [30] and MIQE 2.0's lot-number items [4]. Inferences: in ordinary labs, primer lots, master-mix lots and RT batches live in a notebook or on a tube label and are never joined to the Cq table; nothing in the workflow can therefore attribute a drift to a lot. Gaps: no direct observation of lot tracking practice in academic labs; Reddit threads unreachable.

**Q4. The "7 pm" questions and wishes.**
Takeaway: the same ~25 questions recur for a decade; answers are rules of thumb from strangers. Cited findings: Section 2 (pain inventory) lists them with sources. Explicit wishes found: "I wouldn't want to plot all these on excel!" [18]; authors call for "automated tools to support MIQE compliance" that "scan manuscripts before submission" [1][2]; "we urge researchers to share raw fluorescence data along with analysis scripts that run from raw input to final figures" [5]; "Instrument manufacturers are encouraged to enable the export of raw data" [4]. Gaps: Reddit/X/Bluesky/LinkedIn not reachable, so "I wish" quotes from bench scientists are thin.

**Q5. qPCR among other techniques; decisions it drives.**
Takeaway: qPCR is the cheap confirmatory assay (RNA-seq validation, knockdown check) and the first decision gate before protein work. Cited findings: validation gene counts [61][62][88]; gene expression 31% of buyer applications, diagnostics 22%, copy number 12%, "microarray/miRNA/RNAi validation" 10% [81]. Inferences: because qPCR gates the next experiment, a wrong fold change wastes the downstream western/ELISA/animal work, not just the plate. Gaps: no source found on western/ELISA sequencing decisions; biotech go/no-go not documented.

**Q6. Economics.** Takeaway and findings in 1.6. Gaps: installed base unverifiable; software licence prices unverified; hours of analysis per study unmeasured.

**Q7. Core facilities.** Takeaway: cores sell instrument time and plate assembly, rarely interpretation; clients get Cq tables and are on their own. Cited findings: Penn State "does not provide data analysis services" [36]; UC Davis offers "raw Cq values... or the comparative Cq method... (i.e. Livak or Pfaffl Methods)" and runs client plates for $30–45 [31]; UC Davis QA/QC: director reviews all data, raw curves kept 5 years, logbooks per reagent lot, corrective actions [30]; commercial provider delivers "raw Ct values, ddCt analysis, plus an Excel file" [42]. Inferences: a core cannot tell a client why a run failed unless the raw curves and layout are in a shared, inspectable record; today that is a phone call. Gaps: no ABRF or client-satisfaction survey for qPCR cores found.

## 3. Discoveries (ranked by impact × feasibility × fit)

Each discovery has the seven parts required by the brief. "Record" means qPilot's Analysis Record; "card" means a Decision Card.

### D1. Run triage: "repeat, rescue or ignore" decided by consequence and cost, not by a 0.5-Ct rule

1. **Persona and situation.** The PhD student or technician at 7 pm looking at a finished plate: one NTC at Ct 36.4, two samples with replicate spread 0.9 Ct, one undetermined well. Today they decide by folklore and repeat the whole plate or nothing. Every lab, several times a month (estimate); the questions are the top ResearchGate threads [43][44][45][49][50].
2. **Evidence of the pain.** Rules conflict (0.2–0.5 Ct [44][45], <1.5 Ct [33], SD>0.5 [21], Cq-dependent table [34], "up to two cycles" acceptable to Cq 35 [6]); NTC rules vary (5-cycle gap [65]; ">38" [19][21]; "no impact" if product <30 [50]). Full reruns are triggered by replicate gaps alone ("17 samples... called for a full rerun") [70]. A repeat costs roughly $150–300 and 3–5 person-hours (estimate, 1.6), plus sample. Low-copy noise is Poisson, so fixed rules over-reject at high Cq and under-reject at low Cq [6][4].
3. **What exists today.** Vendor flags (HIGHSD/NOAMP/EXPFAIL) that are "advisory" [63]; qRAT drops replicates above an SD threshold (default 0.3) [8]; VoilaPCR flags >0.5 Ct and NTC gaps (closed, Cq-input, cloud) [64][65]; PlateVision heatmaps (GPL-3) [16]. None of them answers "does this well matter for any conclusion, and what exactly must I re-run?"
4. **The concrete feature.** Input: the study RDML (raw curves, sample/target per well, plate id), plus the group design. Deterministic method, run per questionable well: (a) classify the anomaly: NTC signal with melt peak within ±1 °C of the target Tm = contamination candidate, peak 5–10 °C lower = primer-dimer [65][21]; replicate deviation measured on efficiency-corrected N0 (LinRegPCR) rather than Cq, with the Poisson-aware tolerance of Ruiz-Villalba et al. (expected CV from copy number: CV ≈ 1/√N; 33% at 10 copies) [6] and the Bustin 2025 Cq tiers [10]; (b) a consequence test: recompute every group comparison for the affected gene under the three options (keep / drop well / drop sample) and under "treat NTC level as background subtracted from all wells"; report which conclusions change (this is qPilot's robustness engine applied to one plate); (c) a cost line: wells to repeat (only the affected gene × affected samples, with their NTC and IRC), reagent cost from a per-lab price profile (defaults from [75][86]), hours, and cDNA volume required vs. recorded remaining volume (see sample ledger below). Decision: one card per gene with options "ignore (no conclusion changes)", "drop and record", "repeat these N wells", "repeat plate"; recommendation ranked by impact. Record: anomaly class, evidence (Tm, Cq gap, N0 CV), options evaluated, choice, reason, hash. Agent: `qpcr_triage(study, plate) → list of cards`; `qpcr_repeat_plan(card_id) → well list + cost`. Sample ledger: optional per-sample fields (cDNA volume, dilution, aliquots) from a CSV or ELN export; subtracted per plate.
5. **Feasibility.** Methods are published and permissive to implement: LinRegPCR via rdmlpython (MIT; already in qPilot); Poisson CV and Cq tiers [6][10]; melt-peak detection by negative first derivative of the melt curve (RDML carries melt data) [21][65]; Grubbs/Dixon as optional classical tests (closed-form). Data to validate: the Brintz 2025 CC0 curve set (brief) for anomaly classes; the RDML benchmark (brief). Effort: 2–3 weeks for the triage core, 1 week for the cost/sample ledger. Hardest part: an honest "no conclusion changes" statement at the study level when later plates are not yet run; mitigate by stating the test is conditional on current data and re-running triage when plates arrive.
6. **Why qPilot.** It already holds raw curves, per-well efficiency, the study design and a robustness engine; triage is the robustness engine pointed at one plate before the study is finished. The card/record/MCP pattern makes the repeat decision auditable, which no vendor tool does.
7. **Risks and confidence.** Risk: false reassurance if contamination is systematic (aerosol with the same amplicon across runs [65]); mitigate with D3's control chart. Risk: labs want a rule, not a consequence model; keep a "lab rule pack" fallback. Confidence: high that the pain is real and unserved; medium on adoption (people repeat out of caution and reviewer fear).

### D2. Cq provenance diff: show where the vendor's baseline/threshold choice silently changes the answer

1. **Persona and situation.** Any analyst who exports Cq from QuantStudio/CFX with auto baseline and threshold; also the reviewer who gets only Cq. Every run.
2. **Evidence.** "the Ct values just shift by half a cycle and you never notice" [63]; Cq rises linearly with threshold height and low thresholds inflate replicate variation [6]; MIQE 2.0 names baseline subtraction, threshold use, per-run threshold consistency and smoothing as the four analysis choices that move Cq [4]; manufacturers' software "often yield substantially different results" [5]; Untergasser et al.: Cq-dependent bias from threshold and ignored efficiency, so "reporting Cq and PCR efficiency values is insufficient" [9]; Ruijter et al. 2013 compared 11 curve-analysis methods for bias, precision and resolution across 69 genes [12].
3. **What exists today.** Manual threshold per run in vendor software; LinRegPCR as a separate step requiring raw export with vendor-specific switches ("no baseline subtraction" in Maestro) [47]; qPilot already computes LinRegPCR N0 but does not compare it with the Cq the vendor wrote into the same file.
4. **The concrete feature.** Input: RDML (the vendor's Cq per reaction sits next to the fluorescence readings in the per-reaction data block; verify the exact element names against the RDML 1.3 schema, which I did not open this session) or the vendor xlsx "Results" sheet (Well, Target, CT, Baseline Start/End) joined to the raw curves. Method: for every well compute (i) vendor Cq as exported, (ii) LinRegPCR Cq/N0, (iii) Cy0 (Guescini et al. 2008, BMC Bioinformatics; tangent at the inflection point; a published method I know of but did not fetch this session, so confirm licence-free reimplementation from the paper), (iv) a fixed-threshold Cq at a standardised threshold (one cycle before the end of the exponential phase, per [6]). Add "Cq method" as a new axis in the robustness grid; produce a per-gene card only when the method axis flips a conclusion or shifts a fold change beyond the Cq-tier threshold of D5. Also a "baseline window audit": wells whose vendor baseline end lies inside the exponential phase (the "waterfall"/S-shape cases Thermo describes [19]). Record: per-well table of the four Cqs, the chosen method, reason. Agent: `qpcr_cq_provenance(study) → disagreement summary`.
5. **Feasibility.** rdmlpython (MIT) for LinRegPCR; Cy0 and fixed-threshold are <200 lines; Ruijter 2013's dataset (Vermeulen 2009) availability unknown (gap) but the RDML benchmark suffices for acceptance tests. Effort: 1–2 weeks. Hardest part: reading vendor-specific baseline parameters when only xlsx is available (fields differ by instrument; QuantStudio exports Baseline Start/End [63]).
6. **Why qPilot.** It is the only open tool holding raw curves and a robustness engine in one place; the diff is a natural robustness axis and a natural "explain-any-number" target ("vendor Cq 24.1, LinRegPCR 23.7, why").
7. **Risks and confidence.** Risk: users may distrust the tool when it disagrees with the instrument; present the diff as evidence with MIQE 2.0 item 5.3 ("Method of baseline correction and Cq determination") pre-filled. Confidence: high (methods mature, pain documented).

### D3. Lot and batch drift ledger: Levey-Jennings control charts on the inter-run calibrator and NTC per assay, with lot fields in the record

1. **Persona and situation.** The lab that runs the same 10 assays for months (thesis, biotech programme, core facility). A new primer lot, master-mix lot or RT batch quietly shifts Cq; it is noticed when a cohort "doesn't replicate". Every reagent change (estimate: monthly for an active lab).
2. **Evidence.** Four master mixes on the same cDNA gave 10- to 30-fold spread in ΔCq-derived values; two mixes gave 2- to 5-fold differences in fold change [11]; "a change of master mix can generate different results, especially if the expression changes are marginal" [11]; MIQE 2.0 requires lot numbers for RT reagents and the qPCR kit and says assays must be "verified when changing batches" [4]; the UC Davis core keeps reagent logbooks and compares every new enzyme lot against old lots with controls [30]; 2012 vs 2014 papers from one group differed by ~28-fold and ~45-fold average Cq-derived values "strongly suggest a technical difference" that could not be diagnosed [11]; pooled ΔCq 1.5 between instruments [10]. The ELN guide notes dilution factors "kept in someone's head" [32].
3. **What exists today.** Paper logbooks at good cores [30]; nothing in Excel; qbase/qRAT use calibrators only to scale plates, not to monitor drift [8][25]. Clinical labs run Levey-Jennings/Westgard charts on controls, but research software has none. The brief lists "lab memory... drift alerts by reagent lot" as a headline; this specifies the data model, the statistic and the decisions.
4. **The concrete feature.** Data model: per run, optional fields `primer_lot[target]`, `mastermix_lot`, `rt_batch_id`, `rt_enzyme_lot`, `instrument_id`, `operator`, `plate_type`; entered once per run via a 10-field form, CSV, or read from RDML `experimenter`/`documentation` and the vendor xlsx "Sample Setup" comments; stored in the record (RDML has no formal lot field, so qPilot's record extension carries them and exports a MIQE 2.0 Section 3/4 lot table). Statistic: for each assay, the IRC's efficiency-corrected N0 (and NTC Cq, and per-well efficiency median) plotted run by run; limits from the first ≥10 runs; Westgard rules adapted for research (1-2s warn, 1-3s / 2-2s / R-4s flag); a change-point test (CUSUM) aligned to lot-change dates. Decision card: "primer lot P-0319 shifted IRC by −0.8 Cq (3.2 SD); options: re-validate efficiency with a dilution series, apply a lot offset to plates 7–9 (shown with robustness consequences), or exclude plates". Record: chart points, limits, lot timeline, chosen action. Agent: `qpcr_lot_drift(lab_workspace, assay) → events`; `qpcr_lot_register(run, fields)`.
5. **Feasibility.** Westgard rules and CUSUM are public domain and trivial; IRC per plate is already recommended practice ("≥3 inter-run calibrators" [25]) and present in careful labs [27]. Effort: 2 weeks including the form and charts. Hardest part: getting labs to type lot numbers; mitigate with defaults carried forward from the last run and a barcode/photo capture later.
6. **Why qPilot.** The on-disk shared workspace and hash-chained record make a lab-level longitudinal store natural; per-well efficiency gives a drift signal (efficiency drop) that Cq-only tools cannot see.
7. **Risks and confidence.** Risk: few labs run an IRC on every plate; the chart then runs on NTC Cq and reference-gene Cq only (weaker). Confidence: medium-high; evidence that lot effects are large is strong, evidence that labs will record lots is weak.

### D4. Multi-plate assembly with layout-as-loaded verification and overlap-based between-run correction

1. **Persona and situation.** Any study larger than one plate (most theses): samples or genes spread over 3–12 plates run on different days, sometimes by different people. "QPCR: analyses involving in-between plates?" is a standing ResearchGate question [48].
2. **Evidence.** 6-fold offsets between plates [7]; calibrator-based correction fails when a calibrator well fails and "errors in these calibrators will propagate to all samples on the plate" [7]; qBase asks for ≥3 IRCs [25]; TATAA sells synthetic IPCs [26]; labs choose sample-maximisation layouts to dodge the problem [27]; wrong-well loading is undetectable after the fact [67]; naming mismatches between plate, file and ELN [32]; Maestro treats same-named biological replicates as technical [55].
3. **What exists today.** qbase/qRAT IPC scaling (needs all calibrators to succeed) [8][25]; Factor-qPCR (Ruijter 2015), a standalone program implementing the overlapping-replicate correction and exporting RDML, licence and maintenance status unknown [7]; tidyqpcr for planned layouts (R) [17]; PlateVision heatmaps (GPL-3) [16]. The brief lists "parallel plates", "inter-plate calibrators" and a "plate design assistant" as headlines; this goes to the exact algorithm and the verification step.
4. **The concrete feature.** (a) Plate plan ingestion: a long-format plan (well, sample_id, target_id, prep_type, plate_id) as tidyqpcr defines it [17], or the vendor "Sample Setup" sheet; (b) layout-as-loaded verification: deterministic checks that the data match the plan: NTC wells show no/late amplification, -RT wells are ≥5 cycles later than +RT, each sample's reference-gene N0 is consistent across plates within the expected plate factor (a sample whose reference Cq jumps 3 cycles on one plate is a candidate mis-load), replicate blocks agree, edge wells are not systematically later (row/column effects on a 96/384 heatmap); each failure becomes a card ("wells B3–B5 look like sample 7, not sample 8"); (c) between-run correction by the Ruijter 2015 method: for each plate pair compute the geometric mean of ratios over shared conditions (technical or biological replicates, not only designated calibrators), fill missing pairs by nested geometric means (their Eq. 3), derive a run factor per plate (Eq. 4), divide N0 by it (Eq. 5); report the overlap matrix and recommend "run another plate to fill in the missing overlap" when more than two rounds of substitution would be needed [7]; (d) add "correction method" (none / calibrator / overlap) as a robustness axis. Record: plan, verification results, overlap matrix, factors. Agent: `qpcr_plate_verify(study)`, `qpcr_between_run(study, method)`.
5. **Feasibility.** Equations are explicit in the open-access paper [7]; the plan format is public [17]; heatmap QC ideas are reimplementable without GPL code. Effort: 3–4 weeks. Hardest part: distinguishing a mis-loaded well from a real biological outlier; present as a card with both options and never auto-relabel.
6. **Why qPilot.** Whole-study analysis from raw curves is exactly the precondition for overlap-based correction (it needs N0, not Cq); the record keeps the plan and the as-loaded verification together, which Excel never does.
7. **Risks and confidence.** Risk: labs with no overlap at all (every sample once, one plate per gene) get no correction, only a warning; that is correct behaviour. Confidence: high on method, medium on verification heuristics (need tuning on real mis-load cases).

### D5. Claim guard: minimum reportable fold change from Cq level and replicate spread, with bootstrap intervals bound to the figure

1. **Persona and situation.** PI and student deciding whether "1.6-fold down, p=0.04" goes in the paper; reviewer deciding whether to believe it. Every figure.
2. **Evidence.** "Fold-changes of 1.2- or 1.5-fold are routinely reported as biologically meaningful, even at low expression levels" [3]; Bustin 2025: two-fold reliable only at Cq<25, ≥3-fold at Cq 25–30, ≥4-fold above Cq 30; a 95% CI of 0.71–14.74 for a 3× comparison above Cq 30; six technical replicates needed for 1.5-fold at SD 0.5 Cq; "most studies report fold change or mean Cq values without quantifying uncertainty" [10]; MIQE 2.0 asks for prediction intervals and power calculations [4]; Vandesompele's 25%/10% normalisation error figures [11]; efficiency error of 3% can double a 30-cycle ratio [5].
3. **What exists today.** Nothing in vendor software; Prism error bars on 2^-ΔΔCq; qPilot's GRADE-style confidence and `qpilot plan` (brief) but without the Cq-tier rule or a bootstrap on technical replicates.
4. **The concrete feature.** For each gene and comparison: compute the mean Cq tier of the groups, the technical-replicate SD on efficiency-corrected N0, a non-parametric bootstrap (10,000 resamples over technical replicates within biological replicates) of the group ratio, and compare the observed fold change with the tier threshold (2/3/4-fold) and the bootstrap 95% interval; flag claims whose interval crosses 1 or whose size is below the tier; write the interval into the figure legend and the Study Confidence reasons. Decision card only when a claim fails (options: report as "not resolved at this depth", add technical replicates (number computed from SD, power 80%), or run a dilution series to tighten efficiency). Agent: `qpcr_claim_check(study) → per-claim verdicts`.
5. **Feasibility.** All closed-form or bootstrap; numpy/scipy (BSD). Effort: 1 week. Hardest part: tiers are a proposal from one group [10]; expose them as a named, versioned rule pack rather than hard truth.
6. **Why qPilot.** Per-well N0 and the robustness engine already exist; the "no naked numbers" rule means the interval can be bound to the figure and the methods text.
7. **Risks and confidence.** Risk: authors dislike being told their result is unresolved; present it as a reviewer-proofing step. Confidence: high on need, medium on the specific thresholds.

### D6. Machine-checkable MIQE 2.0: auto-fill what the data can prove, state what it never can, and emit the editable Table 1

1. **Persona and situation.** Author at submission, reviewer at review, core manager preparing a client report. Every paper; 2 of 432 cite MIQE today [2].
2. **Evidence.** The 432-paper audit: RNA integrity 7–10%, efficiency 5–8%, software unnamed in 48–59%, M-values 0.4%, RDML 0%, "impossible to determine whether >90% of qPCR results are reliable"; the authors ask for "automated tools" that "scan manuscripts before submission" and for structured templates [1][2]. Reporting "collapsed" trends (efficiency 13% to 1%, 2019–2024) appear on a vendor page without a traceable source; unverified [87]. MIQE 2.0's Table 1 is downloadable as an editable supplement [4].
3. **What exists today.** Static checklists; Clarida's MIQE score (Cq input; paid); SciScore-style manuscript scanning for other guidelines [2]. The brief lists "auto-filled MIQE 2.0 checklist" as a headline, so this names the items.
4. **The concrete feature.** Classify MIQE 2.0 Table 1 items into three classes and ship the map: (A) provable from RDML + record, auto-filled with values and fact ids: "Storage and submission of raw fluorescence data using RDES or RDML" (the record is RDML), "Method of baseline correction and Cq determination" (LinRegPCR, version), "qPCR analysis program (source, version)", "Frequency and Cq of negative controls" (every NTC/-RT well), "PCR efficiency estimation and method", "Method of target quantity calculation" (efficiency-corrected N0), "Description of replicates", "Repeatability (intra-experiment variation)" (replicate CV per assay), "Dynamic range" if a dilution series is present, "Method of validation of reference genes" (geNorm M/V), "Description of normalization method", "Statistical methods used", "Complete thermocycling parameters including reaction volume" and "Manufacturer and type of qPCR instrument" (RDML carries cycling conditions and instrument fields; confirm element names against the 1.3 schema), "Primer and probe sequences" if the RDML target block carries sequences; (B) fillable only if the lab typed it into the record (D3 lot fields; cDNA priming method; RNA amount; extraction kit; storage); (C) never machine-checkable (sampling procedure, optimisation performed, biological justification). Output: the editable Table 1 with Yes/No, "location of the information", and the engine fact ids; a methods paragraph from the decision log (brief headline) is the companion. Agent: `qpcr_miqe(study) → table + gaps`.
5. **Feasibility.** The checklist text is in the open-access paper [4]; RDML 1.3 schema is public; effort 1–2 weeks. Hardest part: class B fields need a lightweight capture UI (shared with D3).
6. **Why qPilot.** The record already contains most class-A facts with hashes; binding each checklist row to a fact id is the "no naked numbers" principle applied to reporting.
7. **Risks and confidence.** Risk: journals do not enforce MIQE, so authors may not care; the reviewer-critic agent (brief) is the other consumer. Confidence: high on feasibility, medium on demand.

### D7. Core-facility handoff bundle: a client-readable "what happened to your run" page plus the record as the deliverable

1. **Persona and situation.** Core manager and the client PI/student. Cores run the plate and hand back Cq tables; some explicitly do no analysis [36]; the better ones offer Livak/Pfaffl on request [31] and keep raw curves 5 years [30]. Every core run.
2. **Evidence.** Penn State: "the facility does not provide data analysis services" [36]; UC Davis: director "will interface with clients on all aspects... progress, problems, and recommended solutions" and must review all data; data kept "so a third party can assess the results"; corrective actions re-run samples after failed controls [30]; commercial deliverable "raw Ct values, ddCt analysis, plus an Excel file" [42]; prices $30–100 per run [31][35]; EPFL robot-assembles plates, so the layout exists as a machine file before the run [40].
3. **What exists today.** iLab/booking systems, email, Excel. Vendor cloud (Connect) shares vendor files only. No core deliverable includes an auditable analysis or a plain-language failure explanation.
4. **The concrete feature.** A `core` profile in `run --batch` (brief) that takes the client's plate plan and the instrument export, runs D1 triage and D4 verification with the core's rule pack, and emits (i) a one-page client report: run status, which controls passed (NTC/-RT per assay with Cq), replicate quality per assay, efficiency per assay, "wells we recommend repeating and why", in sentences bound to fact ids; (ii) the Analysis Record (RDML + decision log) as the file of record; (iii) a QA line for the core's own logbook (instrument, operator, lots, from D3). Decision: the core staff member approves the report (agent-requests inbox, brief) before release. Agent: `qpcr_core_report(run, client)`.
5. **Feasibility.** Reuses D1/D3/D4; report template is a day's work; the QA/QC guidance [30] is a ready-made specification for what the logbook line must contain. Effort: 1–2 weeks after D1/D4.
6. **Why qPilot.** Local-first (cores cannot send client data to a cloud), shared workspace with locks (core staff and client agent on one record), autonomy levels (core decides what clients may change).
7. **Risks and confidence.** Risk: cores fear liability for interpretation; the report must say "observations", not conclusions. Confidence: medium (no core survey reached; the practice evidence is from SOPs and price pages).

Smaller candidates not developed (evidence thinner): a sample-budget ledger on its own (P13); an RNA-seq-validation module that computes the Pearson correlation between RNA-seq log2FC and qPCR log2FC and flags genes outside the agreement band (P14; evidence [61][62][88]); an Excel-safe export that writes gene symbols as text with a leading apostrophe or as a .csv with quoting (P7; [13]).

## 5. Open questions and what could not be verified

1. **Repeat rate.** No audit or survey of how many research qPCR plates are repeated; the clinical "invalid run" literature was not reached (search budget). Needed for the D1 business case; could be collected by qPilot itself (opt-in commons, brief).
2. **Analysis hours per plate/study.** No measured numbers anywhere; my 30 min–2 h per plate is an estimate.
3. **Installed base.** 80,000 to >250,000 instruments from aggregators without methods [80]; no vendor disclosure found.
