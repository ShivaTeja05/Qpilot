## 1. How this corner of the niche works

### The people and the lifecycle (plain-language model)

Six roles decide whether a tool like qPilot gets used:

1. The bench scientist (PhD student, postdoc, technician) who runs the qPCR instrument. They sit at an instrument PC that belongs to the instrument, not to them. They export a file (CFX .pcrd, QuantStudio .eds/.xls/.txt, RDML) and move it to their own laptop or a shared drive by USB stick, e-mail or network share, then analyse in Excel, Prism, vendor software or a web tool.
2. The lab manager/PI who pays for licences and decides what the lab standardises on.
3. The core-facility director who runs instruments as a fee-for-service operation for many labs, works to standard operating procedures (SOPs), is short of staff and money, and must prove to clients and auditors which raw data produced which figure.
4. The research software engineer (RSE) or bioinformatician who installs, packages and sometimes maintains tools; often on a fixed-term contract.
5. The IT/security officer who enforces "supported OS, patched within N days, no local admin" rules, reviews vendor questionnaires (HECVAT, SOC 2) for anything hosted, and decides what an instrument PC may connect to.
6. The funder/journal (CZI EOSS, Wellcome, NIH, UKRI, JOSS) that decides whether maintenance gets paid for and whether the software counts as a scholarly output.

The adoption lifecycle for an open tool is: discover (paper, forum, colleague, course) → install (the first 15 minutes decide everything) → first result on own data → trust (validation, provenance, a human to ask) → standardise (SOP, training, record-keeping) → sustain (maintainers paid, releases keep working on new OS versions). The evidence for each step is below.

Vocabulary used in this note: SBOM = software bill of materials (machine-readable list of components); SmartScreen = Windows' reputation check that warns on unknown downloaded executables; notarization = Apple's scan-and-ticket step for software distributed outside the App Store; HECVAT = Higher Education Community Vendor Assessment Toolkit (a university security questionnaire for vendors); SOC 2 = a CPA-issued assurance report on a service organisation's controls; DMSP = NIH Data Management and Sharing Plan; pseudonymisation = replacing identifiers with codes while keeping the key separately (GDPR Art. 4(5)); Safe Harbor = the HIPAA de-identification method that removes 18 listed identifiers; SMP = software management plan; FAIR4RS = FAIR principles adapted for research software; CFF = CITATION.cff citation metadata file; JOSS = Journal of Open Source Software; EOSS = CZI's Essential Open Source Software for Science programme; update site = ImageJ/Fiji's mechanism for distributing plugins through the built-in updater.

### Q1. The lab computing reality, and how commercial tools (Prism, Benchling, Clarida, vendor software) are installed and licensed

Takeaway. No quantitative estimate of the fraction of instrument PCs that are offline or locked down could be sourced without web search; the qualitative picture from vendor manuals, an ELN field study and university standards is consistent: instrument PCs are Windows-only, need administrator rights only at install time, are configured in ways IT dislikes (Secure Boot off, no automatic restarts), are often offline and tied to the instrument, and data leaves them by USB, e-mail attachment or network share. Commercial analysis tools are either per-seat subscriptions tied to a user's own devices (Prism) or hosted web apps that carry formal security attestations (Benchling) or EU hosting statements (Clarida).

Cited findings.
- Bio-Rad's CFX Maestro 2.3 user guide: the computer must run "Microsoft Windows 10 (64-bit only), build 1511 or later, with the latest security updates"; "Secure Boot must be disabled on computers running CFX Maestro. Computers running CFX Maestro should be configured so that they do not automatically restart after a system or security update if a run is in progress. See your system administrator for assistance." Installation is from a USB drive and step 2 is "Log in to the CFX Maestro computer with administrative privileges." [48]
- The same guide documents how data leaves the instrument PC: "On connected CFX Duet systems, data can only be transferred via a USB drive"; "Transferring Data Through Email ... The run data are emailed as a .pcrd file"; CFX Opus can transfer "to an attached USB drive or to a shared network folder." Export menus offer Excel, CSV, XML and "Export RDML File ... version 1.1 or 1.0". [48]
- Bio-Rad's product page lists CFX Maestro as Windows-only ("Microsoft Windows 11" is the only OS listed), with a "Security Edition" for "compliance with U.S. FDA 21 CFR Part 11" that adds mandatory login, audit trails, e-signatures, "a hardware protection key, and file encryption"; licences are sold per seat (1-licence and 5-licence catalogue numbers). [51]
- Thermo Fisher's QuantStudio Design and Analysis Software 2 user guide (MAN0018200 Rev. M, 15 October 2025): "The Windows 11 operating system was added to the minimum requirements for customer-provided computers"; the desktop software runs on "Windows 10 (64-bit), Windows 11, or Macintosh OS"; installing Rosetta 2 on macOS begins "Sign in to the computer as an administrator"; importing TaqMan assay files "requires an internet connection"; the software has a separate Security, Auditing and E-signature (SAE) Administrator Console and exports "analyzed data in RDML (Real-Time PCR Data Markup Language) format". [49]
- Thermo's 7500 Real-Time PCR System software-download page carries the instruction: "NOTE: Prior to downloading any software to a computer used for instrument control/operation, please consult with your Service and Support representative." Two PC software lines are still offered: 7500 SDS v1.5.1 (also in a 21 CFR Part 11 version) and 7500 Software v2.3. [50]
- Windows 7 extended support ended 15 January 2020 (ESU to 11 January 2023); Windows 10 Home/Pro support ended 14 October 2025 ("Windows 10 will reach end of support on October 14, 2025"). [52][53]
- Stanford's Minimum Security Standards require endpoints to "Apply security patches within seven days of publish" and "Use a supported OS version"; servers must "Enable host-based firewall in default deny mode"; "University schools, departments, or organizations may impose more restrictive requirements"; the page points researchers to a separate Research Policy Handbook section for research computing systems. [54]
- In a 2017 field study of academic labs trialling electronic lab notebooks, "Lab computers were often offline and tied to specific instruments. Participants were reluctant to use them for notes or cloud software"; laptops were a "hassle of carrying ... around" with risk of contamination; "iPads could work as a shared notebook"; lack of suitable hardware made 12.5% of post-pilot participants stop using the ELN; professional IT support was "pivotal"; users feared being "tied into data formats". [44]
- GraphPad Prism (the de facto statistics/graphing standard in biology labs) is sold as a subscription: academic individual US$260/year, academic group US$520 (2 seats) to US$2,600 (10 seats), student US$142/year, corporate individual US$520/year; a personal licence gives "Access Prism on up to two personal devices" and "may not be shared with other users"; a perpetual licence is US$1,600 (academic) and GraphPad says "We do not recommend this option"; group plans include "Online license management". [38]
- Benchling (hosted ELN/LIMS) publishes a trust page listing "SOC 2 Type 2 attestation", "ISO/IEC 27001:2022 certification", "GxP / 21 CFR Part 11 compliance", alignment to GDPR, CCPA, NIST, C5 and NCSC, and "Annual penetration testing by independent third party"; a security white paper is available through a Trust Portal. [41]
- Clarida (the qPCR incumbent) is a web app (app.clarida.bio) "Priced per experiment": Free €0 for 2 experiments/month; Pay-as-you-go €15 per experiment (prepaid pack from €300); Lab €8 per experiment annual (600–1,200 experiments/year = €4,800–€9,600/year) or €10 monthly; Center "Custom"; "Data hosted in EU" and "GDPR-compliant" marked Yes on all tiers; SSO/SAML and audit log only on Center "on request"; the home page promises "Your data, always exportable" and "Export everything, anytime", with no research-use-only statement found. [39][40]
- Developer-tooling licences that universities trip over: Docker Desktop is free only "for a non-commercial open source project and/or ... a commercial undertaking with fewer than 250 employees and less than US $10,000,000", "Government Entities shall not use Docker Desktop ... without purchasing a Subscription", and education/academia is not mentioned at all. [18] Anaconda's 2024 terms: the Anaconda Repository "requires a license for any use at companies with 200 or more employees" other than educational/academic instructional use; "Miniforge, and any other non-Anaconda installer, is free for everyone"; conda-forge packages remain free. [19]
- Python itself installs per-user on Windows: "You will not need to be an administrator" for the per-user install, which goes to "%LocalAppData%\Programs\Python\PythonXY"; the newer Python install manager "does not support installing runtimes per-machine"; the "embeddable package" is "a ZIP file containing a minimal Python environment ... intended for acting as part of another application". [12] uv's standalone installer puts binaries in "$HOME\.local\bin\uv.exe" on Windows and "~/.local/bin/uv" on macOS/Linux, with winget and scoop options [13]; "uv tool install ruff" installs a CLI into an isolated environment on the user's PATH and "uvx ruff" runs a tool without installing it. [14]

Inferences (mine, labelled).
- Every instrument PC that shipped with Windows 10 or earlier is now outside the "supported OS" rule that security standards like Stanford's impose; the predictable institutional response is network isolation or removal from the network, not upgrading (the vendor explicitly tells users not to download software onto the controller without consulting support, and tells them to disable Secure Boot). Expect many instrument PCs to be offline-by-policy in 2026, and plan for a two-machine workflow (instrument PC → analysis laptop) as the default, not the exception.
- "Admin rights" are needed for the vendor software install but not for day-to-day use; a per-user, no-admin install path for qPilot (Python per-user installer, uv, portable zip) is therefore sufficient for the scientist's own laptop, and a portable zip is the only realistic route onto an instrument PC.
- The incumbents set the trust bar differently: Prism competes on price-per-seat and simplicity; Benchling on attestations; Clarida on EU hosting and per-experiment pricing. A local-first tool cannot show a SOC 2 report (there is no service to audit) and must substitute artefacts that answer the same questions (see Discovery D3).

Gaps.
- No survey or forum data on the fraction of instrument PCs that are offline, on shared logins, or on university rules about local admin rights (my attempts at UCL, Imperial, Cambridge, Edinburgh and UC Berkeley policy URLs returned 403/404; Stanford's standard is the only one I could read).
- FlowJo licensing (dongles/site licences) could not be read from flowjo.com (the overview page has no licence text; pricing is "Request a Quote"). [FlowJo overview page, not listed as a source because it contained no usable facts]
- Prism's activation/offline behaviour is not stated on the pricing page.

### Q2. How open tools won, and how they fail

Takeaway. The winners (ImageJ/Fiji, QuPath, CellProfiler, napari, Galaxy, Bioconductor) share a short list of properties: run without an installer or with per-user installers, ship plugins through a built-in channel, have one place where users ask questions, publish often and build on every platform automatically, and were funded for maintenance (NIH, CZI EOSS, Wellcome, UKRI). Failures are measurable: roughly a quarter of published bioinformatics web servers are unreachable, availability halves over ten years, 28% of published tools could not be installed within two hours, and most were built by people on temporary contracts with no successor.

Cited findings — what the winners did.
- Fiji "is distributed as a portable application": "just download, unpack and start it", with a warning "'Program Files' not recommended!" because Windows "will deny Fiji write permission to its own directory structure" and updates would fail; most downloads bundle a JDK. [20] ImageJ/Fiji update sites: "Anyone can share their extensions" by "creating their own update site", and users "do not need to manually download and install anything". [21] Original ImageJ is public domain (with the caveat that the "waiver of copyright applies only to U.S. law"); ImageJ2/SciJava are BSD-2; Fiji itself is GPL with mixed components. [22]
- QuPath ships a Windows .msi and a "Portable (.zip containing .exe)" that "should not need such privileges" (admin), warns that Windows "tries to protect you from software it does not know" (click "More info" → "Run anyway"), and states it "isn't currently a signed application" because the project lacks resources to "distribute QuPath as a signed/notarized app"; Linux builds have "best efforts made to include all dependencies". [23] QuPath is GPL-3.0, 1.5k stars, 363 forks. [24] It received CZI EOSS grants in Cycle 1 ("QuPath: Open Source Bioimage Analysis and Quantitative Pathology") and Cycle 4 ("QuPath: Boosting Bioimage Analysis for Users & Developers"). [6]
- CellProfiler "is currently referenced more than 2000 times per year"; funded by NIH "R35 GM122547 and P41 GM135019" and CZI grants "2018-192059" and "2020-225720"; distributed as "Pre-compiled builds for Windows and MacOS"; compute-heavy deep-learning tools are "independent plugin modules which are distributed separately from the main CellProfiler program" to avoid dependency problems; it was rewritten from MATLAB to Python 2 in 2010 and ported to Python 3 because Python 2 "reached its official end-of-life in 2020"; the bundled ImageJ was replaced by RunImageJMacro using the user's own ImageJ to avoid "keeping releases of the two programs in sync". [27] Repo: 1.1k stars, 426 forks, badge version 4.2.8. [28]
- napari (BSD-3-Clause, 2.8k stars) offers "A standalone installer for when you want napari without setting up Python first" plus conda-forge and "uv pip install 'napari[all]'" routes, and warns that conda's default solver "may be slow or pick the wrong napari version". [25][26]
- The Scientific Community Image Forum (image.sc) has "approximately 10,000 users in total and more than 15,000 posts per year", 17 community partners (Fiji, ImageJ, CellProfiler, ilastik, scikit-image, DeepLabCut, Bio-Formats, ...); it was created because "many scientists did not know where to start" among per-tool forums; a community partner "links to the forum in its documentation and does not promote separate project channels more prominently"; cross-tool tags are the mechanism for cross-software discussion. [1]
- Galaxy's publication library listed 11,697 publications (Aug 2021); the Galaxy Help forum, launched 29 November 2018 to replace Biostar, had 150–200 monthly active accounts and about 75 new questions and 150 answers per month; "around 100 events per year"; the community conference has had "over 200 participants" every year since 2012. [71] CZI funded "Extending Galaxy for Large-Scale and Integrative Biomedical Analyses" (Cycle 3) and "Automated Generation of Galaxy Tools" (Cycle 6). [6]
- Bioconductor: "3691 total Release Packages"; release "updated twice each year"; Technical and Community Advisory Boards meet monthly; Support Site "18999 active members in the last year", Slack "550 active members". [67] Package review "typically takes between 2 and 6 weeks"; a package must "Not exist on CRAN", include "full documentation and vignettes (including fully evaluated code)"; after acceptance nightly builds run and "It is the maintainers responsibility to check the build reports"; automatic notifications are sent when a package starts failing. [68] CZI funded Bioconductor's build system (Cycle 1), training infrastructure (Cycle 4), Carpentries-style training "including workshops in Africa" (Cycle 6) and GPU CI (Cycle 6). [6]
- Bioconda: "over 12,000 bioinformatics packages" (the page elsewhere says "over 8000"), "over 1800 (as of 2023)" contributors, every package gets an automatic BioContainer; platforms are Linux (x86-64, AArch64) and macOS (x86_64, ARM64) only — no Windows. [69] Mangul et al. found "Tools in package managers such as Bioconda were always installable", versus a 32% failure rate outside package managers, and single-command installs took about 6 minutes. [2]
- Nextflow is Apache-2.0 (3.5k stars) [29]; Seqera's hosted platform has a free "Cloud Basic" tier (3 users, 3 concurrent runs, 250-run history) plus an "Academic Program" offering "free, expanded access", with paid Pro/Enterprise/Validated tiers and published usage rates (CPU US$0.10/hour). [30][85]
- Open Microscopy Environment: "funded by peer-reviewed grants" (current: Wellcome Bioimaging Technology Award 313803/Z/24/Z and UKRI Digital Research Infrastructure UKRI304; earlier BBSRC, Wellcome, NIH Common Fund, Horizon 2020); source under "the GNU General public license or more permissive open source licenses" or a "commercial license" from Glencoe Software; Bio-Formats reads ">140 proprietary file formats". [33] Glencoe sells "OMERO Plus", an enterprise OMERO with an "advanced permissions model" and "multiple SSO standards", and "The licensed Bio-Formats solution". [32]
- Opentrons' robot software is Apache-2.0 and the company "makes robots for biologists" (revenue from hardware; the repo contains the API, app and protocol designer). [34] Posit is "designated as a Public Benefit Corporation", states "data science software should be free and open for everyone" and sells Workbench, Connect and Package Manager alongside open RStudio/Shiny/tidyverse. [31] jamovi is "a free and open statistical spreadsheet" with a hosted Cloud (Guest free; Priority "Priced by region"; Teams "Custom quote" with "on-premise deployment (beta)"). [35] JASP is "an open-source project supported by the University of Amsterdam". [36]

Cited findings — how tools fail.
- Of 36,702 URLs in 51,236 papers (2000–2017), "almost 28% of all resources are currently not accessible"; 41.9% of software referenced before 2012 was unavailable versus 17.5% from 2012 on; 97.6% of GitHub links and 93.0% of SourceForge links were accessible versus 70.3% for other hosts. Of 98 tools tested for installability, 51% were "easy to install" (≤15 minutes) and 28% could not be installed within 2 hours; manuals listed on average 3.9 commands where about 8 were needed; failing tools needed about 70 extra minutes and manual fixes failed for 66% of them; 68% shipped an example dataset; installed tools had significantly more citations (p = 0.032). Recommendations include "Avoid commands that require root privileges", a package-manager install, bundled dependencies, example data with expected results, a quick-start plus full manual, archiving on Zenodo, and a badge server that runs installation tests during peer review. [2]
- Of 2,396 web tools from 2,727 PubMed articles (2010 onward) checked daily for 133 days in 2020: 25.7% were unreachable on day one; availability falls roughly linearly with age ("half-lifetime of 10 years"); 31% were always reachable, 48.4% sometimes, 20.6% never; tools on bare IP addresses (45.6% reachable) or in home directories (54.1%) did worst; non-institutional contact e-mails predicted unavailability; the authors recommend security updates "at least once every six months", containers, pinned dependencies and a domain name. [3]
- Of 927 NAR web services (2003–2009), 72% were still at their published address, 9% completely unavailable, 13% not working as expected; none used a persistent URL or DOI; among 274 responding authors, services were overwhelmingly built by "students and researchers without permanent positions" (abstract: 78%; body: 58% — the paper is internally inconsistent); "For 54% of services, no successor has been found"; high-usability services were cited 1.8× more; top user complaints were non-functional sites (48%), missing example data (40%) and missing documentation (40%). [4]
- A UK survey (n = 417, 15 Russell Group universities): 92% use research software, 70% say "It would not be practical to conduct my work without software", 56% develop their own, and 21% of those have no training. [10]
- The ImageJ 25-year history paper exists [86] but its full text was behind Nature's login and Europe PMC returned server errors; only its abstract ("origins, challenges and solutions") was read.

Inferences.
- The adoption formula that repeats across winners is: zero-admin install (portable or per-user) + a plugin/update channel that does not require reinstalling + a single community venue linked from the docs + automatic cross-platform builds + maintenance money that explicitly pays for docs, onboarding and community (which is exactly what EOSS lists as fundable). qPilot already has the engine and record; the missing pieces are distribution plumbing and community venue, not features.
- Web-hosted-only distribution is the dominant failure mode in this field (decay half-life ≈ 10 years, 1-in-4 dead on arrival). A Hugging Face Space demo is fine as a demo; a local, pip/conda-installable artefact plus Zenodo-archived releases is what survives.
- Bioconda's lack of Windows builds means conda-forge, not Bioconda, is the right conda channel for a Windows-heavy bench audience.

Gaps.
- No fetched source on napari-hub plugin counts, Fiji update-site counts, or the Fiji paper's adoption numbers; no fetched evidence on Discourse vs GitHub Discussions vs office hours as support channels beyond the image.sc paper (which confirms image.sc merged pre-existing Discourse forums).

### Q3. What core facilities and lab managers require before adopting

Takeaway. The only recent, quantitative European survey of core facilities (n = 253 complete, 19 countries) shows a quality gap that a record-keeping analysis tool directly addresses: 72% of facilities have no system to identify the raw data behind a published figure, only 40% of full-service and 10% of self-service facilities have any quality control of data analysis, about a third use management software, and the top constraints are funding and staff. Core staff work to SOPs, bill fee-for-service, and offer training at nearly every step.

Cited findings.
- Kos-Braun, Gerlach & Pitzer (eLife 2020): 1,000 European core facilities invited, 276 forms returned (28%), 253 complete, 19 countries, over 30 facility types. "Traceability: 72% of all facilities lack a system to identify the raw data behind published figures (85% self-service, 63% full-service)." Quality control of data analysis exists in 40% of full-service and 10% of self-service facilities. Data management plans: 30% of full-service and 10% of self-service. Raw-data storage offered by 82% of full-service and 40% of self-service facilities; about 30% offer no storage, "mostly because they believe they should not." About 30% use management software and 34% think it would be useful; about 35% use facility-management software, about 35% data-management software, 4% both. Training/guidance along the research process is offered by 85–99% of respondents. "Lack of funding was the most cited [challenge], followed by lack of staff"; facilities also "reported difficulty with software cost and implementation, and with user cooperation." Involving facility staff in publications was seen as quality-improving by 91%. The authors propose four checkpoints: experimental design, sample quality, data analysis and pre-publication review. [42]
- Meder et al. (EMBO Reports 2016): facility staff are "used to working according to standard operating procedures"; cores are funded by "a combination of user fees and institutional funds" on a "fee-for-service basis"; budgets must cover "data storage, computing capacity" and "investment into new software tools"; "Sustainable costing and pricing models need to be established"; staff "engage in PhD training courses and workshops"; facility involvement can extend to "analyzing data for users and providing plots for publication". [43]
- Core directors want measurable quality: ABRF lists a "CU COres – Metrics Assessing Core Research Facilities" resource (page content "coming soon!") and a monthly "ABRF Lab Operations Management Software Discussions" event; its genomics bioinformatics research group exists. [82]
- The two vendor Part-11 editions (CFX Maestro Security Edition: "mandatory password-protected login, time- and date-stamped audit trails, electronic signatures, a hardware protection key" [51]; QuantStudio SAE console with permanent e-signatures and controlled "Edit RDML Export Destination" permission [49]) show what regulated-adjacent cores already pay for: user roles, audit trail, signatures, controlled export paths.

Inferences.
- A core will not adopt a research tool because it is clever; it will adopt it if (a) it slots into an SOP, (b) it produces a client-facing artefact (report with provenance), (c) it reduces staff time per run, and (d) it can be qualified once per release on the core's own PC. qPilot's hash-chained record is exactly the missing "which raw data produced this figure" mechanism the 72% lack — but only if the record hash is printed on every exported figure and table and verifiable by someone who did not run the analysis.
- Cores are the natural distribution channel for a qPCR analysis standard (they train most users, 85–99%), but they have no budget for software ("difficulty with software cost and implementation") — so the first offer to cores must be free, with a paid support/validation tier later.

Gaps.
- No ABRF survey data on software/data-analysis needs could be fetched; no procurement-threshold figures (the point at which a university requires tender/security review) were found; no qPCR-core-specific requirements documents were read.

### Q4. Funding and sustainability models

Takeaway. The one funder built for maintenance of existing tools, CZI EOSS, has run six cycles (~US$47M by my sum of the cycle amounts, the last co-funded by Wellcome and Kavli) and currently lists no open cycle; its published evaluation philosophy explicitly penalises novelty and rewards maturity, governance, documentation and community roles. Successful open scientific software companies pair an Apache/BSD/GPL core with hosted or enterprise editions (Posit, Seqera, Glencoe), hardware (Opentrons), or university backing (JASP), and licence shocks (Anaconda, Docker) show what not to do.

Cited findings.
- EOSS: "supports software maintenance, growth, development, and community engagement for critical open source tools"; fundable uses include "hiring an additional developer, improving documentation, addressing usability, improving compatibility, onboarding contributors, and convening a community"; cycle sizes: 1: $5M, 2: $3.8M, 3: $3M, 4: $11M, 5: $12.5M, 6: $11.7M "with Kavli Foundation and Wellcome Trust"; Cycle 6 is shown "Closed" and no cycle is listed open. [5]
- Strasser et al. (the EOSS team's "Ten simple rules for funding scientific open source software"): "Beware the novelty bias" (favour mature, widely adopted projects); "Require explicit, transparent governance and roadmaps" (ask for software management plans); maturity signals are "number of contributors; age of repository", documentation, contributor guidelines, a code of conduct and dependencies; downloads and forks "can mislead"; they recommend removing citation-based impact questions; advise long-term archiving (Software Heritage), building on mainstream tools, and candour about technical debt. [7]
- JOSS: free ("JOSS does not charge any fees"), "diamond open access", "a Crossref DOI is minted", reviews "take place openly via public GitHub issues"; submission requires "at least six months of public development history", commits "distributed over 6+ months", an OSI licence file, automated installation ("Dependencies are unclear, and/or installation process lacks automation" is not acceptable), documented API, tests, community guidelines, a statement of need, state-of-the-field comparison, a research-impact statement and an AI-usage disclosure; "Reviewers must install the software and verify its core functionality"; a "Single author with no evidence of community engagement, external use, or collaborative input" is not acceptable. [8][9]
- Zenodo: "Total files size limit per record is 50GB"; "Items will be retained for the lifetime of the repository", tied to CERN's programme "for next 20 years at least"; nightly backups with MD5 checks. [75] GitHub's Zenodo integration "issues a new DOI each time you create a new GitHub release" (public repositories only). [76]
- Software management plans: the SSI guide lists minimum contents (expected outputs, who releases, revision control, licence per output) and question areas (assets, IP/governance, access/sharing/reuse, long-term preservation, resourcing); EPSRC's "Software for the Future" call "explicitly requires software management plans". [73] A Dutch "Practical guide to Software Management Plans" (v1.1, 31 January 2023; Netherlands eScience Center, NWO, TU Delft, Leiden, VU, Radboud UMC) exists on Zenodo. [74]
- Company models: Posit PBC (open core + paid Workbench/Connect/Package Manager) [31]; Seqera (Apache-2.0 Nextflow + hosted platform with free Basic tier and academic programme) [29][30]; Glencoe (commercial licence + OMERO Plus on top of grant-funded OME) [32][33]; Opentrons (Apache-2.0 software, robots) [34]; jamovi (free desktop + Cloud tiers + on-prem beta) [35]; JASP (university-supported) [36]; shinyapps.io's free tier is 5 applications and 25 active hours, Starter US$13/month [37].
- Licence shocks: Anaconda now requires paid licences for organisations with 200+ employees except educational instruction, pushing users to Miniforge/conda-forge [19]; Docker Desktop is paid for government entities and silent on academia [18].
- CellProfiler's funding mix (NIH R35 + P41 centre grant + two CZI grants) [27] and OME's (Wellcome + UKRI DRI + BBSRC + NIH Common Fund + Glencoe commercial licence) [33] are the two best-documented long-lived mixes.

Inferences.
- For qPilot the realistic sequence is: JOSS paper after 6 months of public history → Zenodo DOIs per release + CITATION.cff → demonstrable external use (cores, courses) → EOSS or Wellcome/UKRI maintenance application when a cycle opens (none is open today) → an optional paid tier for cores (validated builds, support SLA) that never changes the Apache-2.0 core (the Anaconda episode shows how fast trust evaporates).
- Because EOSS evaluators discount downloads and citations, the evidence to collect is the kind they say they weight: contributors, dependents (other tools importing qPilot's record format or MCP tools), governance documents, and named institutional users.

Gaps.
- NIH programme specifics (R03/R21/U24 software mechanisms, "Research Software Sustainability" notices) could not be fetched (nih.gov 403); Sloan's programme page returned 403; Wellcome's own software funding pages were not reached; EOSS award sizes/durations are not on the public programme page.

### Q5. IT security questionnaires and data governance: what a local-first tool must document

Takeaway. The questionnaires universities use (HECVAT) and the attestations SaaS vendors buy (SOC 2) are built for hosted services; a local-first tool cannot earn a SOC 2 and must instead publish artefacts that answer the same questions directly: SBOM (NTIA minimum elements), signed and provenance-attested builds, a data-flow statement, a pseudonymisation policy for sample names (GDPR: pseudonymised data remains personal data; HIPAA: medical record numbers and dates are Safe Harbor identifiers and a re-identification code "is not derived from or related to information about the individual"), and a retention statement (US federal awards: 3 years from the final expenditure report).

Cited findings.
- HECVAT (per REN-ISAC) "attempts to generalize higher education information security and data protection questions and issues regarding cloud services", comes in "the original robust version or the lightweight version", was created by the HEISC Shared Assessments Working Group "in collaboration with Internet2 and REN-ISAC". [65] (EDUCAUSE's own HECVAT pages returned 403; the current question count and the on-premise variant could not be verified.)
- SOC: "a suite of service offerings CPAs may provide in connection with system-level controls of a service organization"; SOC 2 covers "Security, Availability, Processing Integrity, Confidentiality, or Privacy"; only licensed CPA firms may issue them and the AICPA warns about unlicensed providers. [66]
- SBOM minimum elements (NTIA, 2021): data fields "Supplier, Component Name, Version of the Component, Other Unique Identifiers, Dependency Relationship, Author of SBOM Data, and Timestamp"; formats "SPDX, CycloneDX, and SWID tags"; practices "Frequency, Depth, Known Unknowns, Distribution and Delivery, Access Control, and Accommodation of Mistakes"; "If the software component is updated with a new build or release, a new SBOM must be created"; "At a minimum, all top-level dependencies must be listed with enough detail to seek out the transitive dependencies recursively"; "Many suppliers, including open source maintainers ... may feel their interests are best served by making SBOM data public." [60]
- PyPI attestations (PEP 740) "bind each release distribution" to "a strong cryptographic digest of its contents" and give "publicly verifiable proof that a package was published via a specific Trusted Publisher" (GitHub Actions, GitLab CI/CD, Google Cloud, ActiveState); two attestations per file. [59]
- Windows SmartScreen: it checks "downloaded files against a list of files that are well known and downloaded frequently. If the file isn't on that list, Microsoft Defender SmartScreen shows a warning"; "If a URL, a file, an app, or a certificate has an established reputation, users don't see any warnings. If there's no reputation, the item is marked as a higher risk and presents a warning to the user." [55] Microsoft's own signing service (Artifact Signing, formerly Trusted Signing) requires "a paid Azure subscription" ("doesn't support free, trial, or sponsored Azure subscriptions"), and even for signed files "SmartScreen reputation builds up automatically. The prompt stops appearing once the file hash has sufficient download history." [56] SignPath Foundation offers "Free Code Signing for Open Source software": "we verify that the binary was built from your open source repository", keys live in an HSM, "Integration in your automated build process is simple". [57] Apple: a Developer ID certificate "lets Gatekeeper verify that you're a trusted developer" and is generated by "the Account Holder of your development team in the Apple Developer Program"; notarization "automatically scans your Developer ID-signed software" and issues a ticket; uploads via "xcrun notarytool". [58] QuPath documents the un-signed experience: Windows "More info → Run anyway", macOS 15 "damaged" warnings worked around via "Open Anyway". [23]
- GDPR pseudonymisation (EDPB Guidelines 01/2025, para. 22): "Pseudonymised data, which could be attributed to a natural person by the use of additional information, is to be considered information on an identifiable natural person, and is therefore personal. This statement also holds true if pseudonymised data and additional information are not in the hands of the same person." Para. 20: additional information "may consist of tables matching pseudonyms with the identifying attributes they replace. It may also consist of cryptographic keys" and "is not to be disclosed to or used by persons processing the pseudonymised data." Para. 53: pseudonymisation "can also constitute an appropriate measure ... for ... scientific or historical research purposes or statistical purposes, in particular in order to ensure respect for the principle of data minimisation, Art. 89(1) GDPR." Para. 131 lists the design steps: identify identifiers, choose cryptographic algorithm or lookup table, choose parameters "like size of group or key length", decide what additional information is retained. [61]
- HIPAA Safe Harbor (45 CFR 164.514(b)(2)) requires removal of, among 18 identifiers, "Names", "All elements of dates (except year)", "Medical record numbers", "Account numbers", "Device identifiers and serial numbers", and "Any other unique identifying number, characteristic, or code"; a retained re-identification code is allowed only if it "is not derived from or related to information about the individual" and the mechanism is not disclosed (164.514(c)). [62]
- US federal award records "must be retained for a period of three years from the date of submission of the final expenditure report" (45 CFR 75.361), longer under litigation/audit. [63]
- NIH Data Management and Sharing Policy (effective 2023): a plan must cover "data type", "related tools, code, and/or software", "standards", "data preservation, access, and timelines", "access, distribution, or reuse considerations", and "oversight". [64] (nih.gov itself returned 403; the article summarising the policy gives the effective date as 2023.01.15, whereas NIH's own date is 25 January 2023 — unverified here.)
- Benchling's published set is the de facto expectation for hosted lab software: SOC 2 Type 2, ISO 27001, pen tests, GxP/Part 11 alignment, a Trust Portal. [41]

Inferences.
- The sample-name field in every qPCR export is a governance hazard: scientists type patient codes, dates and sometimes names into it. A local-first tool that (a) hashes or tokenises sample names with a per-workspace secret key before anything leaves the machine (digests, HF demo uploads, agent prompts), (b) warns when a sample name looks like a date, MRN-like number or e-mail, and (c) stores the key separately from the record, implements EDPB para. 20/131 and respects Safe Harbor's "dates" and "any other unique identifying code" items. Whether a keyed hash of an MRN counts as a code "derived from ... information about the individual" under 164.514(c) is a legal question I could not resolve (HHS guidance pages returned 403) — flag it, do not claim compliance.
- Because the engine never phones home, the honest security statement is testable: an "egress test" that runs the whole analysis with the network disabled and asserts zero sockets opened. qPilot already has an offline test and a threat model; packaging them into a reviewer-facing document is cheap.
- Code signing is the single most expensive trust artefact for a two-person team; SignPath (free for OSS, build-from-repo verified) is the route for Windows, and documenting the Gatekeeper workaround (as QuPath does) is acceptable for macOS until there is budget for the Developer Program.

Gaps.
- HECVAT 4 question count and the existence/contents of an on-premise variant (unverified); SOC 2 Type 1 vs Type 2 definitions (AICPA page did not state them); typical university research-data retention periods (often quoted as 10 years in UK/EU policies — unverified).

### Q6. Human factors: notifications, bench devices, QR codes, voice, time-to-first-result, documentation norms

Takeaway. The "run finished" notification is already a vendor feature (CFX Maestro e-mails a list of users at run completion with the .pcrd data file and a PDF report attached, and suggests sending to a phone's e-mail-to-SMS address), and Slack/Teams webhooks are the established pattern in pipeline software (nf-core's hook_url); what nobody provides is "the analysis is done and here is what needs your judgement". Bench devices are a hardware problem (laptops a hassle; iPads suggested; offline instrument PCs); documentation norms (Diátaxis, Ten Simple Rules) and a ≤15-minute install are measurable adoption levers. No usable evidence was found on QR codes or voice in this session.

Cited findings.
- CFX Maestro: "You can connect CFX Maestro to your outgoing email server to send email notification of run completion to a list of users. You can also choose to attach a data file and an analysis report"; "By default, all recipients receive a copy of the data file as an attachment"; "Select Attach Analysis Report to attach a PDF of the analysis report"; "You might be able to configure the system to send an email notification to your cell phone"; it requires an SMTP server and warns that Gmail/Yahoo users "must enable the setting Allow less secure apps". [48]
- nf-core pipelines (Nextflow) expose "--hook_url": "Incoming hook URL for messaging service. Currently, MS Teams and Slack are supported", alongside "--email" ("Email address for completion summary") and "--email_on_fail". [47]
- Nature's 2017 piece "How scientists use Slack" lists "Eight ways labs benefit from the popular workplace messaging tool" (full text paywalled; 8,915 accesses, 33 citations). [46]
- ELN field study: 99% said ease of use would influence their ELN choice (almost 80% "very important"); 81% rated automatic experiment-data capture important; 74% were concerned about entering data twice; willingness to pay topped out at about US$50/month; over 60% of ELNs were web-based; "iPads could work as a shared notebook"; the article concludes ELNs "cannot currently" fully replace paper. [44] Nature (2018) notes articles have predicted ELN adoption "Since at least the 1990s ... It has yet to happen". [45]
- Time-to-first-result: Mangul et al. define "easy to install" as ≤15 minutes, find that 68% of tools ship example data, and that installability correlates with citations. [2] Taschuk & Wilson: "packages should not require root privileges to set up or use"; "Every package should come with a set of tests for users to run after installation"; "echo all parameters and software versions to standard out or a log file". [80] Lee's ten rules for documentation: "Provide a quickstart guide", "Make a README the project's homepage", "Add a help command to command-line tools", "Write useful error messages" ("what went wrong, what state the software was in, and how to fix it"), "Explain how to cite your software" with "a DOI, a BibTeX entry ... and a CITATION file". [79]
- Diátaxis separates Tutorials, How-to guides, Technical reference and Explanation; adopters quoted include Cloudflare ("our north star for information architecture"), Gatsby and Vonage; it claims use in "hundreds of documentation projects". [78] CITATION.cff is rendered on GitHub repository pages, used by Zenodo to populate deposits and by Zotero's browser plugin. [72] FAIR4RS asks for "a globally unique and persistent identifier", distinct identifiers per version, "a clear and accessible license", "detailed provenance", and data exchange that "meets domain-relevant community standards". [11]
- Hugging Face Spaces: Gradio and Docker Spaces "require a paid plan to create: PRO for personal accounts, Team or Enterprise for organizations. Free personal accounts in good standing can still host up to 2 Gradio Spaces running on ZeroGPU"; default hardware 2 vCPU/16 GB/50 GB non-persistent; a cpu-basic Space "will go to sleep if inactive for more than a set time (currently, 48 hours). Anyone visiting your Space will restart it automatically"; outbound network limited to ports 80/443/8080. [15][16] Gradio "share=True" links "expire after 1 week", route through a proxy that does "not store any data", and the built-in auth "does not offer robust security features". [17]
- Voice/QR: labtwin.com now redirects to LabAthena (LabOperator + Xavo), whose site describes no voice assistant [83]; eLabFTW's documentation pages read here mention neither QR codes nor mobile use [84].

Inferences.
- The notification that changes behaviour is not "run complete" (the vendor does that) but "analysed: 2 decisions need you, 1 plate flagged, confidence Moderate" with a link that opens the exact Decision Card locally — see D2. Because digests may leave the machine, they must carry hashed sample names and the record root hash, never raw sample identifiers.
- Bench devices: the realistic "tablet at the bench" is a phone or tablet opening a read-only, local-network Gradio page or receiving the webhook digest; no gloves-and-touchscreen evidence was found and it should not be designed for without user research.

Gaps.
- No fetched evidence on QR codes on lab records, voice assistants in labs, Thermo Connect/InstrumentConnect push notifications (Thermo pages returned 404), tablet usage rates at the bench, or Slack/Teams usage statistics in labs.

## 3. Discoveries (ranked by impact × feasibility × fit)

### D1. The zero-admin distribution matrix, with a "first result in 15 minutes on a locked-down PC" acceptance test

1. Persona and situation. A PhD student with a university-managed Windows laptop (no admin), a shared instrument PC (Windows 10, offline, admin held by the core), and a Mac at home. They want qPilot on all three without a ticket to IT. A core director wants one artefact they can qualify once and copy to five PCs.
2. Evidence of the pain. 28% of published tools not installable within 2 h; "Avoid commands that require root privileges" [2]; vendor install needs admin and a USB drive [48]; Fiji's and QuPath's success with unpack-and-run distributions and their explicit "not Program Files" / "should not need such privileges" guidance [20][23]; Docker and Anaconda terms make "just use Docker/conda" unsafe advice at universities [18][19]; Bioconda has no Windows builds [69]; HF Gradio Spaces now need a paid plan to create [15].
3. What exists today. Clarida and other web tools (need network; see P4); vendor software (Windows-only, admin, per-seat); academic Ct tools as R/Python scripts (P3). No qPCR tool ships a portable Windows build or a per-user installer.
4. The concrete feature. Ship every release in five forms from one CI pipeline: (a) PyPI wheel with Trusted Publishing attestations [59], installed by `uv tool install qpilot` or `uvx qpilot` [14] (document `pipx` as the fallback); (b) conda-forge feedstock (free, reviewed, auto-built) [70] — not Bioconda, because Windows; (c) Windows portable zip (embeddable Python [12] + wheels + `qpilot.exe` launcher) that runs from `C:\Users\<name>\qPilot` or a USB stick and writes its workspace next to itself in `--portable` mode (Fiji's model [20]); (d) Windows per-user installer (NSIS "RequestExecutionLevel user", no UAC prompt) signed through SignPath Foundation [57]; (e) macOS .zip/.pkg with documented Gatekeeper steps until a Developer ID is affordable [58][23]. Add `qpilot doctor` (environment, versions, checksums, offline egress test, example-study run with expected results) and `qpilot quickstart` (opens the bundled example study and the classroom worksheet). Acceptance test, run in CI on clean Windows 10/11 VMs as a standard user: from download to first Decision Card on the example study in ≤15 minutes with ≤3 commands, zero UAC prompts, zero network access.
5. Feasibility. Python per-user/embeddable builds are PSF-licensed [12]; uv is open source (licence not re-verified here); candidate packagers with permissive licences (unverified in this session, check each repo): pynsist (MIT) + NSIS (zlib) for the per-user installer, Briefcase (BSD-3), Nuitka (Apache-2.0), conda `constructor` (BSD-3) for an offline installer; avoid PyInstaller's GPL-with-exception bootloader given the "no GPL in the distribution" rule. SignPath requires builds verifiable from the public repo [57]. Effort: wheel+attestations 1 day; conda-forge recipe 1–2 days plus review wait; portable zip + launcher 3–5 days; signed per-user installer 3–5 days plus SignPath onboarding; CI acceptance test on Windows VMs 3–5 days. Hardest part: antivirus/SmartScreen false positives on bundled Python executables before reputation accrues [55][56], and keeping lxml/numpy wheels consistent across the five forms.
6. Why qPilot specifically. The engine is pure Python with permissive dependencies (rdmlpython MIT [81]); everything is a file (RDML + record), so a portable mode needs no service, database or registry; the MCP server and CLI already exist, so a headless install on an instrument PC is useful without the UI.
7. Risks and confidence. Risk: maintenance burden of five artefacts for two developers — mitigate by generating all from one `pyproject.toml` and one CI matrix, and by treating conda-forge as community-maintained. Risk: HF Spaces paid-plan change makes the public demo cost money or ZeroGPU-only. Confidence: high that no-admin distribution is a precondition for bench adoption (vendor manuals, Mangul, Fiji/QuPath precedent); medium on the exact tool choices (licences of packagers not re-verified).

### D2. "Analysed — two decisions need you": a watched-folder analyser with Slack/Teams/e-mail digests bound to the record hash

1. Persona and situation. A technician starts a 96-well run at 16:30 and leaves; a postdoc runs six plates over a week; a core analyst processes client runs daily. Today they get the vendor's "run complete" e-mail with a .pcrd attached [48] and then do the analysis by hand the next morning.
2. Evidence of the pain. CFX Maestro's e-mail-on-completion with attached data file and PDF, including phone e-mail-to-SMS, shows demand for notification [48]; nf-core's `--hook_url` for "MS Teams and Slack" shows the webhook pattern is standard in scientific pipelines [47]; labs already live in Slack [46]; ELN users want automatic capture (81%) and entering data once (74%) [44]; but no qPCR tool notifies on the analysis outcome (P12).
3. What exists today. Vendor e-mail of raw data; Thermo Connect cloud (requires connecting the instrument to the internet; details could not be fetched); manual analysis the next day.
4. The concrete feature. `qpilot watch <folder> --study <id> --notify <channel>`: a per-user background job (Windows Task Scheduler per-user task or a tray app; macOS launchd agent) that detects completed vendor exports (size-stable for N seconds, parseable) in a watched folder or the e-mail-attachment download folder, imports them into the study, re-runs the study and the robustness grid, and posts a digest: study name, plates analysed, number of Decision Cards by impact, GRADE-style confidence level and named reasons, a "what changed since last digest" line, and the record root hash and record version — with a deep link `qpilot://study/<id>/card/<n>` that opens the local UI. Channels: Slack incoming webhook (JSON), Microsoft Teams incoming webhook (Adaptive Card JSON), SMTP e-mail (mirroring the vendor's SMTP setup, with the caveat the vendor itself documents about webmail providers [48]), and local OS notifications. Privacy rule (default on): digests contain counts, card titles and hashes only; sample names are tokenised (D3); raw numbers only if the user enables "verbose digest". Everything posted is also written into the record as a `notification` event (channel, timestamp, payload hash), so the record proves what was told to whom. An agent can call `qpcr_study` and then `qpilot notify` through MCP, which is the same code path.
5. Feasibility. Standard library + `requests` (Apache-2.0); file-watching via polling (works on network shares where inotify does not); payload formats are documented by Slack and Microsoft (docs not fetched here — unverified detail). Effort: watch loop + import + digest 3–5 days; webhook/email adapters 2 days; deep links and tray/launchd integration 3–5 days. Hardest part: detecting "export finished" reliably for multi-file vendor exports and partial writes, and running a per-user scheduled task on a managed PC without admin (per-user Task Scheduler tasks normally do not need admin — unverified; test in the CI VMs from D1).
6. Why qPilot specifically. Impact-ranked Decision Cards and the Study Confidence level are exactly the content a one-line digest needs; the hash-chained record gives the digest an integrity anchor no vendor e-mail has; the MCP server means an agent can be the watcher.
7. Risks and confidence. Risk: notification fatigue — mitigate by sending only when a conclusion-affecting card appears or confidence changes, which the impact ranking already computes. Risk: digests leak sample identifiers — mitigated by D3's tokenisation default. Confidence: medium-high on value (vendor and pipeline precedents), medium on uptake of webhooks in wet labs (no usage statistics found).

### D3. The Trust Pack: a reviewer-facing bundle generated by CI for every release (SBOM, signed/attested builds, egress test, data-flow and pseudonymisation statements, security summary, SMP, DOI)

1. Persona and situation. A university IT security analyst receives a "can we install this?" ticket; a data-protection officer asks what happens to patient codes; a core director's QA lead asks for validation evidence; an EOSS or JOSS reviewer asks for governance. Each gets a different document today, or nothing.
2. Evidence of the pain. Hosted vendors publish SOC 2/ISO/pen-test artefacts [41]; universities assess vendors with HECVAT, built for cloud services [65]; SBOMs have a published minimum [60]; PyPI provenance attestations exist and are free [59]; SmartScreen punishes unknown binaries [55]; pseudonymised data remains personal data and the key must be held separately [61]; Safe Harbor lists dates, MRNs and "any other unique identifying ... code" [62]; funders ask for SMPs and governance [7][73]; FAIR4RS asks for identifiers, licence, provenance [11]; JOSS requires community guidelines and verified installation [8].
3. What exists today. qPilot already has hardened XML, path policy, offline test, SBOM, threat model and a visible autonomy policy (per the brief). What is missing is packaging these into artefacts in the shapes reviewers expect, plus the privacy pieces.
4. The concrete feature. A `trust/` directory in every GitHub release and on the docs site, generated by CI: (a) `sbom.cdx.json` (CycloneDX) meeting the seven NTIA fields with "known unknowns" marked [60]; (b) `provenance.json` — PyPI/PEP 740 attestations and SLSA provenance links [59]; (c) `signatures.txt` — SignPath-signed Windows binaries with SHA-256 for every artefact [57]; (d) `egress-test.log` — the offline test output proving zero network connections during a full analysis; (e) `data-flow.md` — one page: what is read, what is written, what never leaves the machine, what optional features (model calls, digests, HF demo) send and to whom; (f) `pseudonymisation.md` plus the feature behind it: sample names are replaced by HMAC-SHA-256 tokens under a per-workspace key stored in a separate file (`workspace/.keys/`, excluded from exports) whenever data leaves the machine; an identifier-pattern warning (dates, MRN-like numbers, e-mails, names from a user-supplied list) at import; a statement that this is pseudonymisation, not anonymisation, citing EDPB para. 22 [61], and an explicit open legal point on 164.514(c) [62]; (g) `security-summary.md` — answers organised under the HECVAT domains once the team downloads the official HECVAT 4 spreadsheet (I could not fetch it), stating plainly that SOC 2 does not apply because no service is operated [66]; (h) `retention.md` — records are plain files; recommended retention ≥3 years after the final report (45 CFR 75.361 floor [63]) or the institution's longer rule; (i) `SMP.md` following the Dutch practical guide's sections [74][73]; (j) `CITATION.cff` + Zenodo DOI per release (concept DOI for the project) [72][76][75]; (k) OpenSSF Best Practices badge (free self-certification) [77] and a `SECURITY.md` disclosure policy. Agents can fetch the Trust Pack through an MCP resource so an institutional agent can pre-screen the tool.
5. Feasibility. CycloneDX generators and vulnerability scanners for Python are Apache-2.0 (cyclonedx-python, pip-audit — licences not re-verified in this session); PyPI Trusted Publishing and attestations are free [59]; SignPath is free for OSS [57]; Zenodo and OpenSSF badge are free [75][77]. Effort: SBOM/provenance/signing in CI 3–5 days; egress test packaging 1 day; data-flow, retention, SMP, security summary documents 3–4 days of writing; pseudonymisation feature 3–5 days (tokenisation, key handling, identifier warnings, export paths). Hardest part: getting the pseudonymisation semantics right (tokens must be stable within a study for linkage but not reversible without the key) and the HECVAT mapping without the official question list.
6. Why qPilot specifically. Local-first with deterministic engine means the strongest claim ("nothing leaves the machine") is testable, not promised; the record already hashes everything, so adding a tokenisation layer is a natural extension; the domain-agnostic core means the Trust Pack generator serves every future plugin (ELISA, western blot).
7. Risks and confidence. Risk: over-claiming compliance — the pack must describe, not certify. Risk: key loss makes tokens unlinkable to samples — mitigate with clear key-backup guidance. Confidence: high that IT and DPOs ask these questions (hosted vendors' published sets, HECVAT's existence, EDPB text); medium on how much it accelerates adoption at a given institution (no procurement-threshold data).

### D4. The core-facility qualification kit: `qpilot qualify`, traceability stamps, an SOP template and a client report

1. Persona and situation. A genomics/qPCR core director with 2–4 staff who processes other labs' plates, works to SOPs, bills per run, and is asked by clients and auditors to show which raw data produced a figure. Weekly.
2. Evidence of the pain. 72% of cores cannot identify the raw data behind published figures; QC of data analysis exists in 40% (full-service) and 10% (self-service) of cores; 30% have DMPs; funding and staff are the top constraints; "difficulty with software cost and implementation" [42]; staff work "according to standard operating procedures" and may "provide plots for publication" [43]; regulated-adjacent cores pay for audit trails, e-signatures and controlled export paths in vendor Security Editions [51][49].
3. What exists today. Vendor Security Editions (single-plate, Windows, hardware key, per seat); LIMS/ELN (no analysis provenance); Clarida Center tier (hosted, audit log "on request") [39]. None produces an installation-qualification document a core can file, nor a cross-plate record the client can verify independently.
4. The concrete feature. (a) `qpilot qualify` — runs the public RDML-consortium benchmark and the example studies on this PC, compares to published acceptance criteria (tolerances per metric), captures environment (OS build, Python, package versions, checksums, SBOM hash) and writes `IQ-OQ-<date>.pdf/.md` with pass/fail, signed by the record hash; re-run per release and per PC; stored in the core's qualification folder. (b) Traceability stamp: every exported figure, table and methods paragraph carries `record:<root-hash-prefix> v<n>` and a `qpilot verify` one-liner; `qpilot verify <file>` works on any machine with the portable build (D1) and confirms the file was produced from that record. (c) SOP template (Diátaxis "how-to" form [78]) following the eLife four checkpoints [42]: experimental design (`qpilot plan` output filed), sample quality (import-time QC and empty-well rules), data analysis (Decision Cards reviewed and reasons logged; autonomy level fixed to "Suggest" for client work), pre-publication review (`records summarise` and confidence level in the client report). (d) Client report template: one page per study — what was analysed, decisions taken and by whom (actor field), confidence level with named reasons, robustness agreement count, record hash, how to verify. (e) Training module: the existing classroom worksheet packaged as a 2-hour core training session (cores already train users along 85–99% of the process [42]).
5. Feasibility. Builds on the existing validation against the RDML benchmark and the record; PDF generation via a permissive library (e.g., ReportLab BSD or WeasyPrint BSD — licences not re-verified here) or Markdown→PDF via the docs toolchain. Effort: `qualify` 1 week; stamps + verify 3–5 days; SOP and report templates 3–4 days; training module 2–3 days. Hardest part: choosing acceptance tolerances that are strict enough to mean something and loose enough across OS/BLAS builds — publish them and version them.
6. Why qPilot specifically. Whole-study analysis plus a hash-chained decision log is already the artefact cores lack; actor = human/agent/system in the record maps directly onto "who decided"; the Study Confidence level is the QC-of-analysis mechanism most cores do not have.
7. Risks and confidence. Risk: cores interpret "qualification" as regulatory validation — the kit must state research-use-only and that IQ/OQ language is borrowed, not certified. Risk: cores' instrument PCs cannot run it (then run on the analyst's PC via D5). Confidence: high on the traceability pain (large survey), medium on cores' capacity to adopt new software given staff shortages.

### D5. The two-machine, offline-first workflow: single-file verifiable study exchange over USB and e-mail

1. Persona and situation. Anyone whose instrument PC is offline or locked (P1): run on the instrument, analyse on a laptop, send to a collaborator or supervisor who has nothing installed.
2. Evidence of the pain. "On connected CFX Duet systems, data can only be transferred via a USB drive"; e-mail of .pcrd attachments [48]; offline instrument PCs [44]; vendor warnings against installing anything on controllers [50]; Windows 10 end of support [52]; "supported OS" rules [54].
3. What exists today. Vendor binary files moved by hand; Excel sheets e-mailed; web tools needing upload; nothing verifies integrity or carries decisions.
4. The concrete feature. `qpilot pack <study>` → one `study.qpilot` zip containing the RDML with results, the hash-chained decision log, versions, checksums, optional raw vendor files, the Trust Pack hash and a `MANIFEST.json`; typically a few MB so it survives e-mail limits (size to be measured). `qpilot verify study.qpilot` (also available as a tiny standalone verifier in the portable zip and as a browser-only JavaScript page that never uploads the file) checks the hash chain and prints the summary. `qpilot import study.qpilot` on the laptop detects duplicates and partial transfers and continues the same study. `--portable` mode (D1) lets a core run the importer and packer directly from a USB stick on the instrument PC without installing anything. Agents call the same pack/verify functions through MCP so an agent can hand a study to a human and prove it is intact.
5. Feasibility. Pure Python, standard library (zipfile, hashlib); the record format already defines the content. Effort: pack/import 3–4 days; verify (CLI + browser page) 3–5 days; portable-mode path handling 2 days. Hardest part: Windows "Mark of the Web"/SmartScreen behaviour for executables run from USB [55] and antivirus heuristics on freshly built Python bundles — test in the D1 VMs.
6. Why qPilot specifically. Everything is already a file with checksums; byte-for-byte re-runnability means the recipient can re-run, not just view; the shared on-disk workspace with locks already anticipates two actors on one study.
7. Risks and confidence. Risk: USB use is discouraged by some IT policies — the format is equally valid over a network share or e-mail. Confidence: high (direct vendor evidence of USB/e-mail transfer; portable precedent).

### D6. Community, teaching and publication plan (a plan, not a feature — included because the task asked for it)

1. Persona and situation. The two developers, 6–18 months out, needing users, contributors and reviewers.
2. Evidence. image.sc works because partners "link to the forum in its documentation and does not promote separate project channels more prominently" and tag cross-tool threads [1]; Galaxy replaced Biostar with its own Help forum and runs ~100 events a year [71]; Bioconductor's support site has ~19k active members and CZI paid for its Carpentries-style training [67][6]; JOSS needs 6 months of public history, tests, docs, community guidelines and a reviewer-verified install [8]; Diátaxis gives the documentation structure [78]; Lee's rules give the README/quickstart/help/error-message checklist [79].
3. What exists today. The classroom worksheet, the Gradio UI, the HF Space (stretch). No venue, no paper, no citation metadata yet.
4. The concrete plan. Month 0–1: CITATION.cff, Zenodo concept DOI, CONTRIBUTING, CODE_OF_CONDUCT, SECURITY.md, Diátaxis-structured docs with a 15-minute tutorial on the example study; GitHub Discussions as the venue (free, searchable) with a public "Decision Card gallery" category where users post anonymised cards; answer qPCR analysis questions on existing forums (ResearchGate, Biostars, r/labrats — not assessed in this session) with links back. Month 3–4: a 2-hour Carpentries-style lesson (the worksheet) offered to two core facilities and one methods course; collect named institutional users. Month 6–7: JOSS submission (criteria above), with the Trust Pack and `qpilot doctor` making the reviewer's install step trivial. Month 9–12: conference presence where core staff are (ABRF, GQ/qPCR congresses — programmes not fetched here); propose an "RDML + Analysis Record" community partner model with RDML consortium tools so that, like image.sc, one venue serves several tools.
5. Feasibility. Everything is free; cost is maintainer time (~1 day/week). Hardest part: sustaining answers on forums while building.
6. Why qPilot specifically. The record and the "no naked numbers" explanations make posted cards self-explanatory, which is what makes forum posts useful to others.
7. Risks and confidence. Risk: splitting a tiny community across venues — pick one (GitHub Discussions) and link everything to it. Confidence: medium-high (patterns are consistent across the winners).

### D7. Funding and sustainability roadmap (a plan, not a feature)

1. Persona and situation. Same two developers, deciding how maintenance is paid for after the hackathon glow fades (P11, P14).
2. Evidence. EOSS funds maintenance, docs, usability and community; ~US$47M over six cycles; no cycle open; Cycle 6 co-funded by Wellcome and Kavli [5]; evaluators discount novelty and citations and weight contributors, repo age, governance, SMP, code of conduct, mainstream platforms and archiving [7]; long-lived projects mix NIH/CZI/Wellcome/UKRI grants with a commercial partner (CellProfiler [27]; OME/Glencoe [33][32]); hosted/enterprise tiers on an open core work for Posit, Seqera and jamovi [31][30][35]; licence shocks destroy trust [19][18]; 54% of services have no successor [4].
3. What exists today. Hackathon prize money (unknown), goodwill.
4. The concrete plan. Keep the core Apache-2.0 forever and say so in a governance document (the Anaconda lesson). Collect EOSS-shaped evidence from month 1: contributors, dependents (tools reading the record format or calling the MCP server), institutional users, training events, JOSS DOI. Apply to the next EOSS cycle (or Wellcome/UKRI software calls) when open, framing qPilot as infrastructure for RDML/record-based analysis rather than "a new qPCR tool". Revenue options that do not compromise local-first: (a) paid "qualified build + support" subscription for cores (validated releases, LTS, response-time SLA — the Trust Pack and D4 make this concrete); (b) training (the D6 lesson, paid for industry); (c) optional hosted multi-user workspace later (jamovi/Seqera pattern), never required. Write the SMP [74] now; it is both a funder requirement and the succession plan.
5. Feasibility. No code; a few days of writing plus ongoing bookkeeping of evidence. Hardest part: timing — EOSS cycles are irregular and none is open [5].
6. Why qPilot specifically. Research-use-only and local-first narrow the commercial options but match exactly what EOSS funds; the domain-agnostic core is the "infrastructure" story funders prefer.
7. Risks and confidence. Risk: no grant cycle opens in 18 months — the core-support tier is the hedge. Confidence: medium (funding landscape changes; NIH/Wellcome specifics unverified).

### Features with direct evidence of adoption value (short list to bake into acceptance criteria)

- Install in ≤15 minutes with ≤3 commands, no admin, example data with expected results, quick-start plus full manual [2][80].
- `--help` on every command and error messages that say what went wrong, what state the software was in and how to fix it [79].
- Portable unpack-and-run build and update without reinstall [20][21].
- Notifications on completion with an attached report (vendor precedent) [48], via Slack/Teams webhook (pipeline precedent) [47].
- CITATION.cff + per-release DOI rendered on the repo page [72][76].
- Nightly cross-platform builds with automatic failure notifications to the maintainer (Bioconductor's discipline) [68].
- Published licences of every component and a clear project licence (FAIR4RS R1.1) [11][22].

## 5. Open questions and what I could not verify

1. Quantitative share of instrument PCs that are offline, on legacy Windows, or shared-login — no survey found; forum evidence not collectable without web search. Suggest a 10-question survey to the first 20 pilot labs.
2. University admin-rights policies — only Stanford's standard was readable; UCL, Imperial, Cambridge, Edinburgh and UC Berkeley URLs failed. Ask pilot sites for their written policy.
3. HECVAT 4 question count, domains and whether an on-premise variant exists — EDUCAUSE blocked fetches; the team should download the official spreadsheet and map the Trust Pack to it.
4. Whether a keyed hash of a medical record number is permissible as a Safe Harbor re-identification code under 45 CFR 164.514(c) — HHS guidance pages returned 403; needs a legal read.
5. NIH software funding mechanisms (R03/R21/U24, software sustainability notices), Wellcome and Sloan programme pages — not reachable; EOSS award sizes/durations not on the public page.
6. Thermo Connect/InstrumentConnect notification behaviour and whether QuantStudio instruments are commonly cloud-connected — Thermo pages 404.
7. FlowJo licensing model (dongles, site licences) — no text on the pages reached.
8. QR codes on records, voice assistants and tablets at the bench — no usable evidence found (LabTwin's domain now points to LabAthena, which sells a lab execution system, not voice).
9. Licences of candidate packagers (pynsist, NSIS, Briefcase, Nuitka, constructor) and of uv/Gradio — stated from memory, must be checked before adoption.
10. Discrepancies to note: Mangul et al.'s installability percentages differ between abstract and discussion (51%/28% vs 49.0%/27.6%) [2]; Schultheiss et al. give 78% (abstract) vs 58% (body) for services built without permanent staff [4]; Bioconda's page says both "over 12,000" and "over 8000" packages [69]; the NIH DMSP summary article gives 2023.01.15 as the effective date whereas NIH's own date is 25 January 2023 (unverified here) [64].
