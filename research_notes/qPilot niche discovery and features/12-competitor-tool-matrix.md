## 1. How this corner of the niche works

### 1.1 The landscape in one paragraph

A working scientist meets qPCR analysis software at four layers. (1) **Instrument software** (Thermo QuantStudio Design & Analysis v2, Bio-Rad CFX Maestro 2.3, Roche LightCycler, Qiagen Rotor-Gene) computes Cq from the raw fluorescence, does basic relative quantification, and exports tables; it is bundled with the instrument, Windows-centric, and the only place where the raw curves are guaranteed to live. (2) **Dedicated analysis products** take Cq tables (and, since September 2026 in Clarida's case, instrument files) and add the published methods: efficiency correction, multiple reference genes (geNorm/NormFinder), inter-run calibration, statistics, MIQE reporting and methods text. The paid incumbents here are **Clarida** (launched January 2026 by the people who wrote geNorm, qBase and MIQE), **GenEx** (MultiD, Sweden, price on request) and, historically, **qbase+** (Biogazelle, acquired by CellCarta in 2021 and "fully deprecated" by 2024). A free beta newcomer, **AnnealIQ**, offers chat-driven analysis of Ct values. (3) **General statistics and graphing** — GraphPad Prism dominates figure-making ($260–520 per seat per year; institutions negotiate ~$100–160 per seat) but has no qPCR-specific workflow. (4) **Open and academic tools** — LinRegPCR / RDML-Tools (the reference raw-curve method, GPL-3 web apps on top of the MIT rdmlpython library), a long tail of R and Bioconductor packages (HTqPCR, ddCt, NormqPCR, qpcR, chipPCR, MCMC.qpcr, qPCRtools, pcr, rtpcr), Python packages (qpcr, rdmlpython), Shiny apps (Click-qPCR, MCTOT, qDATA, repDilPCR), and twenty-year-old Excel macros (REST, BestKeeper, NormFinder.xla, Q-Gene, DART-PCR). Most of layer 4 takes Cq values only, most is GPL, several are unmaintained or offline, and none offers inter-run calibration with error propagation in a maintained, permissive package.

The money flows like this [documented sources in the key-question blocks below]: instrument software is "free" because it is bundled; compliance editions are sold separately (Thermo's 21 CFR Part 11 module lists at GBP 3,885 for a 10-licence line; Bio-Rad's Security Edition needs a USB hardware key); Prism is bought per seat by institutions and labs; Clarida meters per experiment (academic €8–15, commercial €33–60) with a permanent free tier of two experiments per month; AnnealIQ is free during beta; the open ecosystem costs nothing and decays (the R package `pcr` was archived from CRAN on 7 Nov 2025, EasyqpcR was removed in Bioconductor 3.14, tidyqpcr's last push was May 2024, SATqPCR and PCR Miner did not answer on 17 Oct 2026, qbaseplus.com is a parked domain, rtprimerdb.org redirects to a reagent reseller's site).

Where the decisions are made: in the instrument software (threshold/baseline, which wells to omit — Thermo's guide documents clicking-and-dragging points out of the amplification plot to omit outliers), in a spreadsheet (which efficiency to assume, which reference genes, how to combine plates — the 2026 Bio-Protocol workflow still has users copying LinRegPCR efficiencies by hand into Excel), and increasingly in a cloud tool (Clarida logs "decisions"; AnnealIQ's AI "picks the right statistical test"). None of the tools I could document lets the scientist see how many of the defensible analysis choices agree with the conclusion; that position is empty.

### 1.2 Key question: commercial tools (Clarida, AnnealIQ, qbase+, GenEx, Prism, ELN modules)

#### Takeaway
Clarida is a nine-month-old, monthly-shipping, EU-hosted, per-experiment-priced cloud product from the geNorm/qBase/MIQE authors; it is the incumbent for "do my multi-plate, efficiency-corrected, geNorm-normalised qPCR analysis properly" and explicitly positions itself as qbase+'s successor. AnnealIQ is a free-beta, Ct-only, single-user chat tool that itself lists interplate calibration and absolute quantification as things it cannot do. GenEx is opaque on price and version. Prism, Benchling, LabArchives and SciNote have no qPCR-specific analysis that I could document.

#### Inferences
- Clarida's pricing says three things about willingness to pay: academics are expected to pay about a quarter of industry; the buyer is the lab or core, not the individual ("charged by experiments, not by people"); and the free tier (2 experiments/month, full functionality) is designed to make the long tail of students use it for nothing. A free, local tool is therefore not competing on price with Clarida for the long tail; it competes on "no account, no upload, unlimited, scriptable, inspectable".
- Clarida's monthly cadence (nine releases in nine months, instrument-file import landing in September 2026) means any "Clarida cannot do X" claim has a short shelf life. The durable differences are structural: cloud-only, per-experiment metering, closed methods implementation, no public API, raw curves shown but (as far as I can document) not re-analysed with a published per-well method.
- AnnealIQ's "cannot do" list (interplate calibration, multi-user) and its Ct-only input are exactly the gaps a raw-curve, multi-plate, record-keeping tool fills. Its choice of browser-side maths plus a cloud LLM mirrors qPilot's "no LLM in the maths" stance, so "the AI never calculates" is not by itself a differentiator any more.
- qbase+'s death is a textbook lock-in failure: a desktop product with an undocumented workspace format, acquired by a services company, left unmaintained, then withdrawn. Its users now have read-only archives in Clarida. A tool that can re-analyse a qbase+ archive with new settings is a position nobody occupies.

- AnnealIQ: no pricing, company, founders, user count; Product Hunt, YC and Hacker News searches return nothing for it.
- GenEx: no price, version or release date anywhere I could reach.
- Benchling: a "Benchling Analysis" qPCR app may exist behind login; not documented on the public site.

### 1.3 Key question: vendor instrument software (Thermo, Bio-Rad, Roche, Qiagen)

#### Takeaway
Vendor software is where raw curves live and where the first silent decisions (threshold, baseline, omitted wells) are made. Thermo's Design & Analysis v2 is the best documented: relative quantification is built in, HRM is a paid plug-in, exports include RDML and a native TXT with raw/amplification/multicomponent/results sections, and there is a documented command-line application for batch export "intended for advanced users who choose to create or export experiments using a scripting language". Bio-Rad's Maestro does multi-run Gene Studies and reference-gene stability, sells a 21 CFR Part 11 Security Edition with a hardware key, and its RDML export has been reported to violate the RDML zip naming convention. Roche and Qiagen module details could not be verified.

#### Inferences
- The documented Thermo CLI is the cleanest "vendor capture" route in the whole landscape: scriptable, batch, both TXT (with raw and amplification sections) and RDML, on Windows and Mac. Everything a GUI-automation approach would do for QuantStudio can be done with a supported command instead; GUI automation remains necessary only for vendors without such a tool.
- Vendor RDML exists but is not dependable (Maestro naming nonconformance; Thermo "subject to change"); a conformance-and-repair layer with a public per-vendor table is a real, unoccupied, low-glamour position that every downstream tool (Clarida, AnnealIQ, RDML-Tools, R packages) would benefit from.
- Compliance is where vendors monetise software (Security Edition, SAE module, Part 11 module at ~£390 per seat). Research-use tools do not compete there, but "audit trail of decisions" has leaked into research-grade products (Clarida), which suggests record-keeping is valued below the regulated tier too.

#### Gaps
- Roche and Qiagen module lists, add-on prices and export formats; CFX Maestro list price; whether Maestro 2.3's RDML still misnames zip members; what the Connect RQ app computes.

### 1.4 Key question: open and academic tools (web, R/Bioconductor, Python, Shiny, Excel, Galaxy)

#### Takeaway
There are dozens of open tools, almost all Cq-input, mostly GPL, mostly single-author, and a visible fraction is dead or dying: `pcr` was archived from CRAN on 7 Nov 2025 because "issues were not corrected despite reminders"; EasyqpcR was removed in Bioconductor 3.14; tidyqpcr (rOpenSci-reviewed, Apache-2) has not been pushed since May 2024 with 27 open issues including QuantStudio import, RDML import, geNorm and Cq-from-curves; SATqPCR and PCR Miner were unreachable; the LinRegPCR Windows program "will no longer be updated". The living core is LinRegPCR web / RDML-Tools (GPL-3 apps on an MIT library, pushed May 2026) plus a handful of CRAN packages with a few hundred downloads a month. Newcomers in 2024–2026 (qDATA, Click-qPCR, MCTOT, rtpcr, GeneQuantify, repDilPCR, HeraNorm) each solve one slice — statistics on ΔΔCq, censored Ct, dilution-replicate, reference genes — and each paper's introduction is a statement of the same pain: spreadsheets are "error-prone and time-consuming", other tools "require a certain level of computational skills", vendor software is "proprietary, commercially licensed, platform-specific".

### 1.5 Key question: user sentiment, who pays for what, and what they refuse to pay for

#### Takeaway
Direct review sites gave me nothing (G2 empty, TrustRadius 403, Capterra not reachable), so sentiment here is triangulated from issue trackers, support threads, tool papers, vendor FAQs and institutional licence pages. The consistent complaints are: Excel is error-prone and slow; vendor software is a black box; combining plates is a manual, doubtful step; efficiency is rarely measured and tools that need it are rarely used; R packages need skills most bench scientists lack. What people demonstrably pay for: Prism seats (institutions negotiate to ~$100–160/seat/year), Clarida Lab/Center (per-experiment, with cores and biotech as the paying segments), vendor compliance editions. What they expect for free: instrument analysis software (bundled), reference-gene calculators (RefFinder, Clarida's free geNorm, NormFinder), and now AI chat analysis (AnnealIQ beta).

### 1.6 Key question: lock-in, data access, cloud adoption, discontinued tools

#### Takeaway
Lock-in in qPCR is mostly format lock-in (EDS, PCRD, IXO, qbase+ workspaces), softened by RDML exports that exist on paper but are inconsistently implemented. The discontinued tools died for ordinary reasons — acquisition by a non-software company (qbase+), a single maintainer leaving (pcr, EasyqpcR, SATqPCR?, PCR Miner?), 32-bit Windows dependencies (REST 2009, NormFinder.xla), and domain lapse (RTPrimerDB). Cloud adoption is pushed by vendors (Thermo Connect free Individual edition; Clarida cloud-only; AnnealIQ cloud account) but every cloud tool I checked had a data-residency caveat (Clarida EU-only; AnnealIQ US-only; RDML-Tools deletes after 3 days; MCTOT on shinyapps has no persistence).

#### Cited findings
- Thermo RDML/TXT export and CLI: see 1.3 [19][20]. Thermo Connect Individual free (25 GB), Enterprise "Any file-based instrument", organisations "own their data" [22]. Thermo export specifications "subject to change" [19].
- Bio-Rad Maestro RDML export misnames zip members per rdmlpython issue #3 [44]; Security Edition encrypts files so they "cannot be opened or edited by another program" [17].
- qbase+: deprecated 2024, undownloadable, workspace folders with kernel.xml/experiment.xml, "One Windows update away from losing all your qbase+ data", Clarida import read-only [5][6]; domain parked [13].
- RTPrimerDB: domain repurposed by a reseller [82]. SATqPCR: connection refused [83]. PCR Miner: unreachable [84]. QPCR (TU Graz): empty page [85]. REST 2009: 32-bit dependency [38]. NormFinder.xla: 32-bit Office only [40]. BestKeeper: password-by-email ZIP, Excel 2003 instructions [39]. pcr: CRAN-archived 2025-11-07 [61]. EasyqpcR: removed in Bioconductor 3.14 [74]. LinRegPCR Windows: no longer updated [46].
- Cloud caveats: Clarida "Data hosted in EU" on all plans [2]; AnnealIQ "infrastructure is US-based", deleted sessions purged within 24 h, account deletion within 30 days [10]; RDML-Tools server "stores the data for a maximum of 3 days" [46]; MCTOT "Shinyapps.io does not provide persistent storage" [50]; Click-qPCR "does not store or record any uploaded data on the server's hard drive" [54].
- Pabinger 2014 on RDML adoption and vendor black boxes [48]; web-LinRegPCR on undisclosed melt-curve analysis [46].

#### Inferences
- A local-first tool's strongest lock-in argument is not privacy rhetoric but the documented history: the three most-used dedicated products of the 2010s (qbase+, REST, LinRegPCR Windows) are all dead or frozen, and the only artefact that survived is the open file format (RDML) and the open library (rdmlpython). Betting the record on RDML plus a hash-chained log is the right bet; the risk is RDML exports from vendors being nonconformant, which argues for the conformance layer in Discovery 2.

- No documentation of Thermo Connect's actual adoption or complaints; no Roche/Qiagen export specifics.

## 3. Discoveries (ranked by impact × feasibility × fit)

### D1. The open qbase+ successor: import qbase+ archives and run the qBase model (inter-run calibration + error propagation) on them, re-analysable

1. **Persona and situation.** Any lab that bought qbase+ between 2010 and 2023 (academic and biotech; the product shipped with Bio-Rad Security Edition licences) and now has a workspace folder it cannot open after the next Windows update. Clarida will take the archive, read-only: "We don't reanalyze, recompute, or modify anything." The scientist who wants to re-run an old experiment with validated reference genes, or merge it with new plates, has nowhere to go. Frequency: once per legacy project, but each is a thesis chapter or paper.
2. **Evidence of pain.** "As of 2024, qbase+ has been fully deprecated", "no longer available for download anywhere" [6]; "One Windows update away from losing all your qbase+ data" [5]; Clarida's import is explicitly read-only and free [5]; the Bio-Rad SKU still promises a qbase+ licence [17]; qbaseplus.com parked [13]. Size unknown (gap): qBase 2007 is cited 3,262 times [59], which bounds the user base loosely.
3. **What exists today.** Clarida read-only import (cloud, no re-analysis); nothing open reads the workspace. The qBase method itself (efficiency-corrected RQ, geometric-mean normalisation, run-to-run calibration factors, error propagation) is published and open [Hellemans 2007, PMC1852402] but, per Pabinger, error propagation lives in only five tools and none of the maintained permissive R/Python packages implements inter-run calibration [48][61][80].
4. **Concrete feature.** `qpilot import qbase <workspace_dir>`: parse kernel.xml and Projects/<p>/Experiments/<e>/experiment.xml (file names documented by Clarida [5]; schema undocumented — needs sample files), map runs, samples, targets, Cq, reference-gene flags and plate layouts into RDML 1.4 (sample types, inter-run calibrator samples); then a method card "qBase relative quantification (Hellemans 2007)" implementing: RQ = E^ΔCq per target with per-target efficiency, normalisation factor = geometric mean of reference RQs, calibration factor per run from shared inter-run calibrators, SE propagation as in the paper's equations; Decision Cards: which samples are the inter-run calibrators (auto-detected from qbase+ sample types, confirmable), which reference set, what efficiency source (qbase+ stored value vs assumed 100% vs new dilution series). Record: provenance of the qbase+ import (file hashes, qbase+ version strings found in XML), the card decisions, parity report. Agent: `qpcr_import_qbase(path) → study_id`; `qpcr_study(study_id, method="qbase2007")`.
5. **Feasibility.** Method: open-access paper with explicit formulas (Genome Biol 2007, CC-licensed article; the maths is not copyrightable). Format: XML, undocumented; need 2–3 donor workspaces (ask on the qPCR listserv / the hackathon's scientist contact; Clarida's page confirms kernel.xml/experiment.xml). Libraries: lxml (BSD), rdmlpython (MIT). Effort: 1 week for the reader once sample files exist, 1 week for the method card + parity tests, 2 days for the import card UI. Hardest part: obtaining real qbase+ workspaces and reproducing qbase+'s exact numbers (its efficiency and error handling had options).
6. **Why qPilot.** The record, cards and robustness grid are domain-agnostic; this adds a Cq-input study type (needed anyway, see D5) and the qBase method card; the "every reasonable analysis" grid naturally includes "qbase+ settings as saved" vs "qPilot defaults" so the scientist sees whether the old conclusion holds.
7. **Risks and confidence.** The population is finite and shrinking; Clarida could add re-analysis at any time; format reverse-engineering without samples is impossible. Confidence: medium (pain documented, size unknown).

### D2. Vendor-export conformance and batch-capture kit (Thermo CLI first), with a public per-vendor RDML conformance table

1. **Persona.** The lab "data person" or core-facility manager with 20–200 EDS/PCRD files per month who needs them in one standard form; also every downstream tool author (RDML-Tools, Clarida, AnnealIQ, R packages) who currently hand-patches vendor quirks.
2. **Evidence.** Thermo documents a CLI that batch-exports EDS/SDS to TXT (Header, Sample setup, Raw, Amplification, Multicomponent, Results sections) and to RDML zip, "intended for advanced users who choose to create or export experiments using a scripting language", with specifications "subject to change" [19]; the desktop guide confirms RDML export is limited to "standard curve, relative standard curve, and comparative Ct analysis" experiment types and is a permissioned "controlled function" under SAE [20]; Maestro RDML zip members are misnamed (rdmlpython #3) [44]; "Not all qPCR machines export RDML" [46]; Pabinger: "Only a very limited number of tools support the direct use of export files" [48]; import requests sit open for years in tidyqpcr (#134, 2021) and pcr (#29) [80][61]; AnnealIQ's main pitch is "auto-detects Bio-Rad, ABI, Roche, or generic formats" [8].
3. **What exists.** Per-tool ad hoc parsers (Auto-qPCR reads QuantStudio xls/txt; AnnealIQ CSV detection; RDES-TableShaper for tables; RDML-Validate for schema). No one publishes which vendor/version exports validate, what is missing (e.g., melt data, sample types, efficiency), or a repair path. GUI automation ("GhostDriver") is on qPilot's roadmap; for QuantStudio it is unnecessary because the CLI exists.
4. **Concrete feature.** (a) `qpilot capture thermo --cli <path> --in <dir> --out <dir>`: wraps the documented command-line application to export TXT + RDML for every EDS in a directory; parses the TXT sections by header (column order is user-configurable, so parse by name); stores software version, export settings file hash and the CLI guide revision in the record. (b) `qpilot rdml check <file>`: validates against RDML 1.1–1.4 schemas, repairs known vendor nonconformances (zip member naming; missing experimenter/dateMade; migrates to 1.4 via rdmlpython), reports what the file lacks for a full analysis (no raw fluorescence → raw-curve engine unavailable → falls back to Cq mode, D5). (c) A public conformance table (vendor × software version × what validates × what is missing), maintained in the repo with anonymised fixture files contributed under CC0. Decision for the scientist: accept repair / use Cq-only / request re-export with raw data. Agent: `qpcr_capture(dir)`, `qpcr_rdml_check(file)`.
5. **Feasibility.** Thermo CLI documented for Windows and Mac (requires D&A v2 installed on that host; the capture step therefore runs on the instrument PC or a lab Windows/Mac box, not necessarily on the analyst's laptop). RDML schemas are public (RDML consortium, GitHub); rdmlpython already migrates versions (MIT). Fixtures: RDML consortium examples; CFX files need donors (the rdmlpython issue implies one exists in its tests — check licence). Effort: Thermo wrapper 3–5 days; checker/repair 1 week; table ongoing. Hardest: Bio-Rad PCRD is proprietary and needs Maestro to export; Roche/Qiagen unverified.
6. **Why qPilot.** Its engine starts from RDML; the record already hashes inputs; the "Ladder of Control" (file → CLI → COM → macro → GUI) puts the CLI rung where it belongs, and the conformance table is the kind of public good an open project can own and Clarida/AnnealIQ cannot.
7. **Risks.** Thermo may change or withdraw the CLI (their guide says specifications may change); redistributing vendor files is not allowed (fixtures must be lab-donated); the Security Edition restricts export destinations. Confidence: high for Thermo, medium for Bio-Rad, low for Roche/Qiagen.

### D3. A maintained, permissive engine-as-library with a tidy output schema and an R bridge (fill the hole left by `pcr`, EasyqpcR, tidyqpcr and the stale rdmlpython wheel)

1. **Persona.** The bioinformatics core analyst or R-literate postdoc who scripts qPCR analysis today with `pcr`/`qPCRtools`/`tidyqpcr` and found `pcr` archived in November 2025, tidyqpcr frozen since May 2024, EasyqpcR gone, and rdmlpython's PyPI wheel three years behind GitHub. They do not want a GUI; they want a function that returns a long table they can pipe into their own models.
2. **Evidence.** CRAN: `pcr` "removed … issues were not corrected despite reminders" (2025-11-07) [61]; Bioconductor: EasyqpcR removed in 3.14 [74]; tidyqpcr: last push 31 May 2024, 27 open issues, Roche-only input [80]; rdmlpython PyPI 1.7.2 (May 2023) vs GitHub main (May 2026), still 1,851 downloads/month [44][45]; demand signals: qpcR 1,320, chipPCR 598, ctrlGene 449, PCRedux 363, MCMC.qpcr 351, qPCRtools 251 CRAN downloads/month [68]; HTqPCR ~3.5k distinct IPs in 2025 [69]; GitHub "qpcr" 1,269 repos, top data-analysis repos 13–60 stars [79]; Kleinschmidt qpcr issue (Sep 2026) asking whether the package computes efficiencies [77]; rtpcr's own comparison table lists only Cq-input R packages [53].
3. **What exists.** Permissive: rdmlpython (MIT, raw curves, no study-level statistics), RDML (R, MIT, import only), qPCRtools (MIT, Cq), PCRedux (MIT, features), tidyqpcr (Apache, stalled). GPL: qpcR, chipPCR, nondetects, MCMC.qpcr, pcr, Kleinschmidt qpcr, GeneQuantify. None gives a maintained, permissive path from raw curves through reference-gene selection to group contrasts with a stable output schema.
4. **Concrete feature.** Publish the engine as the installable library it already is, plus: (a) a frozen **tidy schema** `qpilot-tidy-v1` (one row per well: study_id, plate_id, well, sample, group, target, sample_type, Cq, N0, eff_well, eff_assay, flags[], excluded_reason, method_version, record_hash) exported by `qpilot export --tidy study.csv` and returned by `qpcr_export_tidy` over MCP; (b) a 200-line R package `qpilotr` that shells out to the CLI (no reticulate dependency) and returns tibbles, with one vignette reproducing a Vermeulen contrast; (c) a documented `qpilot.engine.analyse(rdml_path, settings) -> StudyResult` Python API with semantic versioning and a CHANGELOG; (d) an upstream pull request to rdmlpython offering a release workflow so PyPI stops lagging. Decisions stay in the cards; the library exposes `decisions_pending()` so scripts can see what a human still has to answer. Record: schema version and library version are already facts.
5. **Feasibility.** Days for the schema and export (engine facts exist), one week for `qpilotr` + CRAN-compatible structure (no CRAN submission needed initially; GitHub install), one day for the PyPI PR. Licences: all permissive. Hardest part: committing to maintenance — the exact failure mode of `pcr`; mitigate with CI matrix (3 Python versions), pinned deps, and a "two-maintainer" rule.
6. **Why qPilot.** Apache-2.0 engine on MIT rdmlpython; MCP already exists; domain-agnostic core means the tidy schema generalises to the next plate assay.
7. **Risks.** R users may prefer native R; shelling out is clunky on Windows; maintenance load. Confidence: high (gap is documented by archive dates and issue trackers).

### D4. Censored non-detect card: make "what does Undetermined mean?" an explicit, robustness-tested decision (deeper spec of the roadmap headline)

1. **Persona.** Any scientist with low-abundance targets (cytokines, lncRNAs, pathogen loads) where a third of wells return "Undetermined" or Cq > 35; cores reporting detection to clients.
2. **Evidence.** MCTOT 2025: complete-case tools force removal or imputation that "may bring unnecessary bias and decrease statistical power"; Qiagen's web tool "substituted Ct 50 values with the tool's maximum Ct cutoff 40" [50]; `nondetects` has lived in Bioconductor for 12.5 years (GPL-3) [73]; Thermo flags "Unexpected Cq Status" and "Replicate group outlier" but leaves the decision to the user [20]; Pabinger scored "NA handling" as a core criterion [48]; AnnealIQ and Clarida do not document a policy [8][1]; sibling note 01 reports a ResearchGate thread "Is Ct values above 35 in RT-PCR run acceptable or not" [88]. Internal measurement: the project's Vermeulen demo has 323 undetermined wells of 24,576 (reported from the project memory, not re-measured here).
3. **What exists.** `nondetects` (EM-based imputation under a detection-limit model; GPL-3, so reimplement from McCall et al. 2014, Bioinformatics — DOI to be confirmed), MCTOT (rank-based two-group test and Cox-type regression treating Ct ≥ cutoff as censored; R code in the paper's S1 Text, licence of the code unstated, article CC0) [50], Tobit regression (standard). None is wired to a per-gene consequence display or to a robustness grid.
4. **Concrete feature.** A Decision Card "Non-detects (n wells, k genes)" with options: drop (current default), censor at cut-off with a rank-based two-group test (Zhuang 2025) or Tobit on ΔCq, impute by EM (McCall 2014), set to cut-off (Qiagen-style, flagged "not recommended"); each option shows per-gene fold-change and p-value consequences from the engine; the chosen rule becomes a new axis of the robustness grid (so the "holds under x% of analyses" number includes non-detect policy). Inputs: RDML wells with no Cq or Cq ≥ user cut-off; sample type NTC excluded. Record: policy, cut-off, counts per gene/group. Agent: `qpcr_decide(card="nondetects", option=...)` (existing call).
5. **Feasibility.** Methods are published; Tobit via statsmodels (BSD) or a 50-line likelihood; rank test from the MCTOT paper; EM imputation reimplemented from the paper (1–2 weeks total). Validation data: nondetects package example data (licence to check), Vermeulen (CC BY 4.0). Hardest: making the explanation plain enough that a student understands why "drop" inflates effects when non-detects are group-specific.
6. **Why qPilot.** Only a tool with a robustness engine can show the conclusion's sensitivity to the non-detect policy; only a record can make the policy reportable (a MIQE 2.0-relevant item).
7. **Risks.** Statistical subtlety; small-n groups with many censored values give uninformative tests; must not become a way to "pick the policy that gives p < 0.05" — the escalation rule (conclusion-flipping choices are human) and the logged reason mitigate this. Confidence: medium.

### D5. Cq-only mode with an explicit "efficiency assumption" card (the entry door for the majority who only have Cq exports)

1. **Persona.** The bench scientist whose instrument software exported a results table (QuantStudio TXT/XLSX, Maestro CSV, LightCycler text) and who never had, or cannot get, raw curves or RDML; also the qbase+ migrant (D1) and the AnnealIQ/Click-qPCR user who already has a tidy Cq CSV.
2. **Evidence.** Every 2021–2026 open tool and both AI products take Cq only [8][49][51][53][54][55]; Bustin & Nolan: efficiency is "very rarely" measured and ΔΔCq without it is "a clear indicator that the data are suspect" [86]; the 2026 Bio-Protocol still hand-copies LinRegPCR efficiencies into Excel [47]; `pcr` users have asked for efficiency correction since 2020 [61]; Thermo's RDML export is limited to certain experiment types [20], so some runs only ever yield tables.
3. **What exists.** Cq calculators that assume 100% (qDATA, Click-qPCR, Auto-qPCR), tools that accept user-typed efficiencies (AnnealIQ Pfaffl, rtpcr, GeneQuantify) with no record of where the number came from, and no tool that shows the consequence of the assumption.
4. **Concrete feature.** `qpilot run --cq table.csv --map` accepting the Thermo TXT results section, Maestro "Quantification Cq Results" CSV, generic long CSV (sample, target, group, Cq) and RDES/RDML without fluorescence; a Decision Card "Amplification efficiency source": assume 100% (not recommended), per-assay from a dilution series in the same study (RDML std samples), per-assay from the lab's assay passport (roadmap "lab memory"), or a sensitivity band (1.80–2.00) swept by the robustness grid; per-gene consequences shown; geNorm and contrasts run exactly as in raw mode; the Study Confidence level is capped at "Low" when efficiency is assumed, with the reason named. Record: input table hash, column mapping, efficiency source and values. Agent: same `qpcr_study` with `mode="cq"`.
5. **Feasibility.** Parsing: Thermo TXT sections documented [19]; Maestro CSV headers from donor files; mapping UI: a 10-row preview. Methods: Pfaffl 2002 (open), geNorm (already implemented). Effort: one week. Hardest: honest handling of technical replicates (Click-qPCR and `pcr` both stumble here) — reuse the kinetic/replicate rules from the raw path.
6. **Why qPilot.** The cards, grid, record, confidence level and MCP are input-agnostic; adding Cq mode multiplies the addressable users (everyone with a table) while keeping the raw-curve path as the "upgrade" the tool nudges toward ("ask your core for the RDML").
7. **Risks.** Cannibalises the raw-curve story in the pitch; must be visibly labelled as the weaker path. Confidence: high (pain and gap both documented).

(Two further positions are real but are positioning rather than features and are left in section 1: the unlimited-free-local price point against Clarida's metering, and the core-facility batch/QC segment that Clarida's Center tier and Thermo's Enterprise edition both target but no open tool serves.)
